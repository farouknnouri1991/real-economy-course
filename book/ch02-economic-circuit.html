4  Chapter 2 — The Economic Circuit and How We Measure It

National accounts, flows, and the limits of GDP

“Not everything that can be counted counts, and not everything that counts can be counted.” — attributed to William Bruce Cameron

The Map

The previous chapter defined economics as a discipline and laid out the tools of economic reasoning. Before opening the firm, the market, or public policy, a shared language is needed: how money, goods, and incomes circulate among the actors of an economy, and how that activity is measured. This chapter supplies the dashboard that will be consulted from here to the final page.

It presents first the economic circuit, the round of expenditures and incomes that links households, firms, the state, banks, and the rest of the world, and shows why this round rests on invisible institutions. It then analyses the measurement of production through value added and GDP, with the three equivalent readings of the same aggregate. It introduces the distinctions without which no serious economic discussion is possible: nominal versus real, stocks versus flows, and the inflation measure. It closes on a decisive question: what GDP does not say: the invisible labour, the informal economy, distribution, and the environment.

The guiding thread of this chapter
  1. The economy is a circuit, not a collection of isolated individuals. One agent’s expenditure is another’s income; the loop holds through invisible institutions and can seize up through insufficient effective demand.
  2. Measuring production requires rigour. Value added eliminates double-counting; GDP can be read three equivalent ways; and the nominal/real distinction separates genuine wealth from monetary illusion.
  3. No measure is neutral. Choosing what to count is already choosing what counts.

Part I — The Economic Circuit: The Round of Expenditures and Incomes

What economists call the economic circuit is simply the round of expenditures, incomes, and production that connects all actors in an economy. To analyse this round, actors are sorted into categories, each with a distinct role: households, firms, the state, banks and finance, and the rest of the world.

This round functions concretely only because there exist also rules of coordination, institutions: contracts, accounting norms, legal rules, commercial practices, that translate mutual trust into reproducible acts. When a firm pays wages, a supplier delivers on credit, a bank extends a working capital loan, or a household buys imported goods, each of these acts rests on rules that extend well beyond the immediate parties. Without them, every economic relationship would be unique and non-reproducible, and the modern economy would not exist.

The circuit is your day

In the morning, Karim takes a taxi to work in Casablanca; he spends, the driver earns income. He works at a textile firm, which will pay him a wage at the end of the month. At lunchtime he buys a sandwich from a street vendor, who will in turn pay her supplier. In the evening, his rent transfer reaches his landlord, who uses it to repay a mortgage to his bank. In one day, the same dirham has passed from hand to hand, becoming in turn an expenditure for one and income for another. Multiply by 37 million people and you have the economic circuit. Nobody orchestrates it; it holds through millions of small gestures linked by trust and rules.

Five agents, five roles

Five broad categories of agents play distinct roles in the circuit, and understanding their asymmetries is as important as knowing their labels.

Households supply labour, receive incomes (wages and distributed profits), and choose between consuming and saving. But they are not simply “consumer wallets”: they own firms, directly or indirectly through financial assets: shares, bonds, pension funds, life insurance policies. This ownership is the source of the second type of income: profit distributed to those who hold titles to firms’ earnings.

Firms produce, invest, hire, set prices, and organise internal coordination. They are controlled by their owner-households and their managers, a relationship governed by conventions of corporate governance that Chapter 3 analyses in depth. The firm is the circuit’s active agent: it is firms that decide when to invest, when to hire, and when to cut. These decisions propagate through the entire circuit.

The state taxes, spends, regulates, and redistributes. It creates the legal framework within which all other conventions are established: property rights, the enforceability of contracts, monetary stability, and the system of social protection that transfers income across the cycle of individual life.

Banks and finance transform household savings into credit for firms and sometimes households, manage liquidity and risk, and intermediate the circuit’s monetary dimension. They are the key intermediaries, and their behaviour, whether to expand or contract credit, shapes the amplitude of cycles.

The rest of the world exports, imports, and circulates capital, linking the national economy to a globalised set of conventions. For Morocco, this fifth agent is structurally decisive: the economy runs a persistent current account deficit, financed by remittances from Moroccans abroad, tourism receipts, and foreign capital inflows. This dependency on external financing is one of the structural vulnerabilities that economic policy must manage.

Households: passive owners, savers, workers

A household in economics is not a family in the anthropological sense. It is a unit of decision: one or more individuals who jointly decide how much to consume, save, and work. This simplification allows the household to be treated as a single agent, despite the real conflicts that exist within couples and families, about spending priorities, risk tolerance, or the allocation of domestic labour.

Household income comes from two radically different sources. Wages: the household supplies labour to the firm, which employs and remunerates it. This is income one “earns” through negotiation, job search, and skill development; the household is here relatively active. Distributed profits: the household owns claims on firms through financial titles; a share of profits accrues to it proportionally. It has done nothing to earn this income as owner, beyond holding the title and waiting.

These two sources explain a macroeconomic reality that is often overlooked: in aggregate, households are net savers. They receive more income than they spend, because the profits they receive as shareholders must go somewhere. They could consume them; in practice, a large part is saved, feeding the financing of investment throughout the economy.

As owners, households are largely passive. A shareholder holding 0.001% of a listed company has no power over investment decisions, hiring, or pricing. She receives a dividend, or not. If she dislikes the management, she can sell, but she cannot direct. Real control belongs to managers, boards, and large funds. Even the owner of a small family business faces this asymmetry: if she manages it herself, she becomes a manager and shifts roles; if she delegates, she reverts to passivity. The distinction between ownership and control is one of the central questions of firm theory, explored in Chapter 3.

The firm: the active agent of the circuit

This passivity of households contrasts with the activity of firms. The firm is asymmetric relative to the other agents for three reasons that are consequential for macroeconomics.

It initiates investment expenditure. Machines, software, buildings, logistics infrastructure: all of this is decided by firms before being financed. When a firm anticipates a fall in demand and postpones its investment plans, distributed incomes fall, consumption contracts, and demand actually falls: the prophecy becomes self-fulfilling. This is why investment is the most volatile component of GDP and the primary driver of cyclical fluctuations.

It bears irreversibilities. Unlike households, which adjust consumption relatively quickly, the firm carries fixed costs (factories, equipment) and path dependencies (accumulated learning, long-term contracts, reputation). These irreversibilities amplify cycles in both directions: in upturns, prior investment commitments generate momentum; in downturns, firms with heavy fixed-cost structures face acute pressure.

It coordinates. Through hierarchy, contracts, payment terms, and inventory management, the firm organises thousands of decisions that, without it, would need to pass through as many separate markets. This coordination function is why firms exist at all, a question to which Chapter 3 returns in depth.

The circularity and its blockages

The loop expenditure → income → expenditure is not a smooth merry-go-round. It has two simultaneous faces: a real side (goods and services circulating) and a monetary side (payments and receipts). These two faces can fall out of phase.

A firm can sell goods (a positive real flow) yet face a cash shortage if its clients pay on ninety-day terms. The real flow is positive; the monetary flow is blocked. This is why payment delays, working capital management, and access to short-term credit are not operational details: they are at the heart of firm survival, particularly for Morocco’s SMEs, which typically face longer payment cycles from large corporate clients than from their own suppliers, creating a structural squeeze.

Circuit leakages are the points where the loop jams. When savings do not transform into investment, through hoarding or debt repayment, expenditure falls without productive justification. When imports rise without compensating exports, national income “leaks” abroad. When taxes are collected but not re-injected through public spending, or when banks stop lending, financing is severed. And when expectations turn pessimistic, firms and households simultaneously defer expenditure, creating exactly the contraction they feared.

The foundational Keynesian point is this: an insufficiency of effective demand can break the loop even when productive capacity exists. The economy can remain blocked below its potential, not from lack of resources but from lack of spending. This mechanism is developed in depth in Chapter 9 on unemployment and fiscal policy.

Part II — Measuring Production: Value Added and GDP

How does one measure what an economy produces? The answer passes through a simple but subtle concept: value added.

Value added: eliminating double-counting

The price of bread, step by step

A farmer sells wheat for 100 dirhams. A miller transforms it and sells flour for 160 dirhams: he has added 60 dirhams of value. A baker buys the flour for 160 dirhams and sells bread for 300 dirhams: she has added 140 dirhams. How much wealth has been genuinely created? Not 100 + 160 + 300 = 560 dirhams, which would count the wheat three times over, but 100 + 60 + 140 = 300 dirhams, exactly the price of the final bread. Value added is the honest way to count: at each stage, only what has newly been contributed. The formula is simple: VA = Revenue − Intermediate Inputs, where intermediate inputs are the goods and services purchased from other firms.

Why not simply add up the revenues of all firms? Because the same thing would be counted multiple times. Wheat is counted when the farmer sells it, again when it is in the flour, again when it is in the bread. Value added corrects this double-counting by retaining, at each stage, only what was genuinely added. The sum of value added across all firms in the economy reconstitutes GDP.

The three readings of GDP

Gross Domestic Product — the sum of value added across all resident producing units, can be read three equivalent ways. Three perspectives on the same reality.

Approach Formula Question answered
Production GDP = ΣVA + product taxes Where is wealth created?
Income GDP = Wages + Profits + Net taxes How is wealth distributed?
Expenditure GDP = C + I + G + (X − M) Who purchases the final output?

These three readings are identities: they coincide by construction of the national accounts. The firm appears in each: as a production unit, as a distributor of incomes, and as the agent that decides investment expenditure. The equivalence is not approximate, it is exact, because every act of production is simultaneously an act of income distribution and an act of supply that will be met by some form of final demand.

Consider a three-firm economy: Firm A (farming) sells wheat for 100 dirhams with no intermediate inputs, adding 100 dirhams. Firm B (the mill) buys 100 dirhams of wheat and sells 160 dirhams of flour, adding 60. Firm C (the bakery) buys 160 dirhams of flour and sells 300 dirhams of bread, adding 140. Total value added is 300 dirhams. If wages across all three firms total 200 dirhams and profits total 100 dirhams, the income approach also yields 300. And if households buy 300 dirhams of bread as the economy’s sole final good, the expenditure approach confirms it. Three descriptions, one reality.

The circuit’s accounting identities

The circuit condenses into a small number of identities that every economist carries:

\[\text{GDP} = C + I + G + (X - M)\]

\[\text{National savings } (S) = \text{GDP} - C - G = I + (X - M)\]

This expenditure identity is true by construction: everything produced is either consumed by households, invested, spent by the government, or exported net of imports. In a closed economy, this implies the remarkable result that national savings equals investment. In an open economy, the identity becomes S − I ≡ X − M: a country that imports more than it exports must finance this deficit through an inflow of foreign capital. This is the logic of the current account deficit, analysed in Chapter 14.

Morocco: an open economy with a structural deficit

Morocco invests more than it saves domestically, and finances the difference through remittances from Moroccans Residing Abroad (MRE), tourism receipts, and foreign capital inflows. According to Bank Al-Maghrib, the current account deficit has oscillated between 2% and 5% of GDP over the past decade. This structural external financing need makes Morocco’s macroeconomic stability sensitive to shocks in any of these inflows, as the COVID-19 crisis of 2020 demonstrated dramatically, when tourism collapsed and remittances initially contracted.

An equally important structural feature: Morocco’s informal sector represents between 30% and 40% of GDP according to the HCP. It absorbs a massive share of employment and directly competes with formal firms, which bear taxes, social contributions, and regulatory obligations that informality evades. This duality is not an accident: it reflects real barriers to formalisation, administrative costs, limited credit access, compliance burdens, and weighs on investment and productive upgrading among SMEs.

Part III — Nominal and Real: The Distinction That Changes Everything

When Morocco’s GDP “increases by 6%”, is this because more goods and services are being produced, or simply because prices have risen? Confusing the two leads to serious errors that recur throughout public economic debate.

The trap of monetary illusion

A nominal quantity is measured at current prices, the prices prevailing at the moment of measurement. A real quantity is corrected for inflation, measured at constant prices. The difference is not technical: it separates genuine wealth from illusion.

The pay rise that was not one

Fatima’s employer announces, with evident satisfaction, a 4% salary increase. Good news? Not automatically. If prices have risen 6% over the same year, she can actually buy less than before with her new salary: her purchasing power has fallen by approximately 2%, despite the larger number on her payslip. Her nominal salary has risen; her real salary has fallen. This is monetary illusion: rejoicing at a larger number without asking what it buys. All of economic reading requires this reflex: always ask whether a figure is measured in today’s dirhams or in real purchasing power.

To move from nominal to real GDP, one divides by a price index called the GDP deflator:

\[\text{Real GDP} = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Deflator}} \times 100\]

The deflator measures the average price evolution of everything produced. Real growth, the only growth that matters for welfare, is the growth in GDP once price increases are removed. A country whose nominal GDP rises 10% with 10% inflation has not grown at all in real terms: it produces the same goods and services, sold at higher prices.

Stocks and flows: photograph versus film

A final foundational distinction, source of countless confusions in public debate: a flow is measured over a period, annual GDP, a monthly wage, quarterly investment. A stock is measured at a point in time, public debt on 31 December, a firm’s capital, a household’s accumulated savings.

The bathtub

Imagine a bathtub. The water level at any given moment is a stock, accumulated public debt, household wealth, a firm’s capital. The flow of water from the tap and the drain are flows, revenues coming in, expenditures going out. One cannot add a level and a flow rate, just as one cannot add public debt (a stock) and the annual deficit (a flow). But the deficit of each year fills the bathtub of the debt. The deficit is the film; the debt is the photograph taken at the end of each year. Confusing the two is confusing a photograph with a film.

Morocco’s public debt is a stock, currently around 70% of GDP. The annual fiscal deficit is a flow, which each year adds to or subtracts from that stock. A deficit of 5% of GDP with nominal GDP growth of 5% leaves the debt-to-GDP ratio roughly unchanged. A deficit of 5% with 2% growth raises it. These arithmetic relationships, simple but consequential, underpin much of fiscal policy debate.

Part IV — Inflation: Measuring the Loss of Monetary Value

Inflation is the general and sustained rise in the price level. It is not the increase in the price of a particular good, a tomato spike after a drought, but the overall movement that erodes, month by month, what a dirham can purchase.

How it is measured: the consumer price index

The HCP monitors each month the price of a representative “basket” of what an average Moroccan household consumes: food, housing, transport, health, clothing, communications. The evolution of the cost of this basket gives the Consumer Price Index (CPI), and its annual rate of variation is the inflation rate.

The household basket

Imagine the typical trolley of a Moroccan family: bread, oil, tea, sugar, the rent, transport, phone credit, some clothing. The HCP virtually fills this trolley each month and observes what it costs. If the same trolley cost 1,000 dirhams last year and 1,030 dirhams today, inflation is 3%. The figure seems abstract but it describes something concrete: by how much has ordinary life become more expensive for an average family. And since each family has a different basket, “average” inflation can be mild for those consuming goods whose prices have not moved and severe for those spending mainly on what has risen sharply. Moroccan households that spend heavily on food and fuel faced effective inflation well above the 2022 headline rate of 6.6%.

The faces of inflation

Inflation has several origins, and diagnosing which is operating matters enormously for policy. Demand-pull inflation arises when aggregate expenditure exceeds productive capacity: too much money chasing too few goods. Cost-push inflation originates in rising production costs, energy, wages, imported inputs, that firms pass through into their prices; this is what Morocco experienced in 2022, when global energy and grain prices surged in the wake of the Russian invasion of Ukraine. Imported inflation strikes open economies dependent on world markets: when oil and wheat prices rise internationally, Morocco imports their inflation directly, regardless of domestic demand conditions.

Bank Al-Maghrib targets low and stable inflation, around 2%. Not zero, because a slight inflation lubricates the economy, it facilitates adjustments in relative prices and wages, and distances the economy from the opposite danger of deflation, where falling prices encourage the deferral of purchases and can trigger a depressive spiral. The monetary policy mechanisms through which BAM pursues this target, the policy rate, reserve requirements, communication strategy, are analysed in Chapter 7.

Part V — What GDP Does Not Say

GDP is a precious instrument. But it is an instrument, not a truth. Confusing it with a nation’s well-being is one of the most common errors in public economic debate, and the confusion is sometimes convenient for those who wish to avoid asking harder questions.

Four blind spots

Unpaid labour. Domestic work, raising children, caring for elderly relatives, managing a household, keeps society functioning but does not appear in GDP, because it is not paid. Carried out overwhelmingly by women, it represents an immense invisible economy. A revealing paradox: if one were to hire paid workers for these same tasks, GDP would rise, even though the actual work performed would not have changed. The paradox exposes a conceptual problem: GDP measures monetary transactions, not economic activity.

The informal economy. In Morocco, between 30% and 40% of activity partially escapes the official accounts. Measured GDP therefore understates real production, and official statistics see only part of what people actually experience and produce. The HCP has developed satellite estimates, but by definition the informal economy resists complete measurement. This means that growth figures in Morocco must be interpreted with awareness that a significant share of economic reality is systematically undercounted.

Distribution. GDP per capita is an average. If all the growth accrues to a few, the average rises while the majority stagnates. A country can “grow richer” according to GDP while most of its inhabitants experience no improvement. Morocco’s growth record over the past two decades illustrates this tension: respectable average growth rates have been accompanied by persistent structural unemployment, deep rural-urban inequalities, and a gender participation gap that removes much of the female labour force from paid activity. The tools for measuring distribution, the Lorenz curve, the Gini coefficient, income shares, are developed in Chapter 15.

The environment and resource depletion. GDP counts production but not the destruction that accompanies it. Overexploiting an aquifer, exhausting a fishery, polluting a river: all of this can raise GDP today while destroying tomorrow’s wealth. GDP adds what is produced without subtracting what is damaged. For Morocco, where rain-fed agriculture depends on fragile aquifer systems and the phosphate extraction model consumes non-renewable national patrimony, this blind spot is not merely an academic concern.

The warning that was there from the start

The person who contributed most to creating GDP as a statistical aggregate in the 1930s issued the warning himself. Simon Kuznets told the United States Congress in 1934 that “the welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measurement of national income.” Nearly a century later, the warning remains as valid as ever. GDP answers the question “how much do we produce?” It does not answer “are we living better?”, “for whom?”, or “at what cost to the future?”

The indicator that constructs what it measures

Consider what happens when policymakers adopt GDP growth as their primary target. This choice, embedded in international institution reports, bond market assessments, electoral cycles, transforms GDP from a measurement tool into an objective. Policies are selected, budgets allocated, and political legitimacy claimed on the basis of this single figure. But because GDP aggregates market transactions, policies that increase monetised activity, even environmentally destructive or distributing gains very narrowly, raise the target indicator. The measurement shapes what is pursued. The choice of what to count is, already, a political act. An economy that produces more but distributes worse and degrades its natural base is not obviously doing better, but GDP will say it is.

These limitations do not render GDP useless. On the contrary, it remains the single most powerful summary statistic for short-run economic monitoring: it is comparable across countries, measured consistently over time, and responsive to cyclical conditions in ways that matter for policy. The point is to use it with clarity about what it measures and what it does not, supplementing it with the indicators of inequality, human development, environmental sustainability, and subjective well-being that together give a fuller account of economic performance.

Conclusion: Counting Is Already Judging

This chapter has laid the shared language that all subsequent economic analysis will require. Three essential acquisitions.

The economy is a circuit, not a collection of isolated individuals. One agent’s expenditure is another’s income; the loop holds through invisible institutions and can seize up through insufficient effective demand. Understanding this circularity means understanding why an economy can stall even when productive resources exist, why the problem is sometimes not capacity but coordination.

Measuring production requires rigour. Value added eliminates double-counting; GDP reads three equivalent ways; and the nominal/real distinction separates genuine wealth from the illusion of rising prices. Without these tools, one confuses a price increase with a production increase, a higher nominal wage with higher purchasing power.

No measure is neutral. GDP illuminates some things and obscures others: invisible labour, the informal economy, inequality, the environment. Choosing what to count is already choosing what counts. The lucid economist wields her instruments knowing their blind spots, and is suspicious of analyses that treat the available measure as the only relevant one.

The next chapter opens the black box of the circuit’s most active agent: the firm.

What this chapter should leave with the reader

The economic circuit connects five agents (households, firms, the state, banks, the rest of the world) through the round expenditure → income → expenditure. Households own but are largely passive; the firm initiates investment and coordinates: it is the active agent.

Value added (VA = Revenue − Intermediate Inputs) eliminates double-counting. The sum of all value added equals GDP.

GDP reads three equivalent ways: production (ΣVA), income (wages + profits + taxes), expenditure (C + I + G + X − M). The three coincide by construction.

Nominal versus real: a real quantity is corrected for inflation. A nominal increase can conceal a real decline in purchasing power, monetary illusion is pervasive in public debate.

Inflation is measured by the CPI (the HCP’s household basket). Bank Al-Maghrib targets approximately 2%. It can arise from demand pressure, cost shocks, or import prices, and diagnosing which matters enormously for the appropriate policy response.

GDP does not say everything: it ignores unpaid labour, the informal economy, distribution, and environmental degradation. It is a useful instrument, not a measure of well-being.

Further Reading

Kuznets, S. (1934). National Income, 1929–1932. Report to the US Congress. The document in which the creator of national income accounting issued his famous warning about the limits of the measure he had helped build.

Stiglitz, J., Sen, A., and Fitoussi, J.-P. (2009). Mismeasuring Our Lives: Why GDP Doesn’t Add Up. The contemporary reference on GDP’s limits, commissioned by the French government. Accessible and wide-ranging.

Keynes, J.M. (1936). The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money. For the concept of effective demand and the blockage of the circuit. Chapters 8–11 are the core of the macroeconomic mechanism introduced here.

Bank Al-Maghrib. Rapport annuel (latest edition). Current account balances, inflation data, monetary aggregates, the primary empirical reference for Morocco’s macroeconomic position.

HCP. Comptes nationaux and Indice des prix à la consommation. Rabat. Morocco’s national accounts and price indices, freely available online at hcp.ma.
