17  Chapter 15 — Inequality, Distribution, and Redistribution

Who gets what, why it matters, and what can be done

“Between the strong and the weak, between the rich and the poor, it is freedom that oppresses and the law that liberates.” — Henri Lacordaire (1848)

The guiding thread of this chapter
  1. Inequality is not one thing. Income inequality, wealth inequality, inequality of opportunity, territorial inequality, and non-monetary inequality (education, health, gender) are related but distinct phenomena requiring different measurement instruments and different policy responses.
  2. The Gini coefficient is a starting point, not a conclusion. It is sensitive to middle-distribution changes and insensitive to extremes. The Palma ratio, the Atkinson index, and wealth distribution data reveal dimensions that the Gini obscures.
  3. Piketty’s r > g is not a law of nature but a structural tendency of capitalism: when the rate of return on capital persistently exceeds the growth rate, wealth concentrates over generations. Reversing this tendency requires institutions, not just growth.
  4. Morocco’s inequalities have identifiable and partially correctable sources. Colonial structural legacy, market rents, labour market segmentation, unequal access to credit and human capital: each mechanism has a corresponding policy response.
  5. Redistribution after the fact is less powerful than pre-distribution. Reforming market structures that generate excessive rents (competition policy, labour market reform, transparent public procurement) is more efficient than taxing the resulting inequality after it is produced.
  6. Morocco’s social protection reform of 2021-2025 is the most important domestic policy change of the decade, and its success depends on administrative infrastructure (the Registre Social Unifié) and fiscal capacity that are themselves works in progress.

The Map

This chapter addresses the question that underlies all the others: who gets what from the economy, and is that distribution justifiable? The preceding chapters built the analysis of production (firms, markets, finance), macroeconomic stabilisation (fiscal and monetary policy), structural transformation (growth, industrial policy), and international integration (trade, balance of payments). This chapter assembles those threads around the distributive question: how do the riches that the economy generates distribute themselves between individuals, classes, regions, and generations?

The chapter advances in four movements. First, the measurement toolkit: Lorenz curves, Gini coefficients, Palma ratios, and the critical distinctions between income, wealth, and opportunity inequality. Second, the structural sources of Moroccan inequalities: colonial legacy, market rents, labour market segmentation, and unequal access to capital. Third, redistribution instruments and their limits: fiscal systems, social transfers, public services, and the concept of pre-distribution. Fourth, the political economy of inequality: who benefits from existing structures and who has the resources to defend them.

Part I — Measuring Inequality: Tools, Indices, and their Limits

The Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient

The most widely used measure of income inequality is the Gini coefficient, derived from the Lorenz curve. The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of the population, ranked from poorest to richest. Perfect equality, every person receiving an equal share, would produce a diagonal straight line. The Gini coefficient is defined as twice the area between this equality line and the actual Lorenz curve:

\[G = 1 - 2\int_0^1 L(x)\,dx\]

where \(L(x)\) is the Lorenz curve. A Gini of 0 represents perfect equality; 1 represents maximum inequality (one person receives all income). In practice, Gini coefficients for income range from approximately 0.25 in the most equal advanced economies (Nordic countries) to above 0.60 in the most unequal developing economies (South Africa, Brazil).

Morocco’s Gini coefficient for consumption expenditures (the most reliable available measure given data limitations) was approximately 0.39-0.40 according to HCP’s 2019 survey. This places Morocco in a middle position globally, more equal than Brazil (0.53) or South Africa (0.63), but considerably more unequal than France (0.29), Tunisia (0.33), or South Korea (0.31).

The Gini coefficient has two well-known limitations that matter for policy design. First, it is most sensitive to changes in the middle of the distribution, making it relatively insensitive to extreme concentration at the top or extreme poverty at the bottom. Second, the same Gini can correspond to very different distributional shapes: a society where the rich are moderately richer than the middle and the middle is moderately richer than the poor can have the same Gini as one with extreme poverty and extreme wealth, provided the middle is distributed accordingly.

Beyond Gini: the Palma ratio and wealth inequality

The Palma ratio, the ratio of the top 10%’s income share to the bottom 40%’s share, addresses one of the Gini’s limitations by focusing directly on the distributional extremes. Gabriel Palma observed that the middle 50% (deciles 5-9) tends to capture a relatively stable 50% of income across countries; most cross-country variation in inequality comes from the shares of the top 10% and the bottom 40%. Morocco’s Palma ratio is approximately 2.1 according to HCP estimates, meaning the richest 10% receive roughly twice as much income as the poorest 40% combined. For comparison: France is approximately 1.0, South Korea 1.2, Brazil 2.8.

The Atkinson index incorporates an explicit normative parameter \(\varepsilon\) measuring inequality aversion:

\[A(\varepsilon) = 1 - \frac{1}{\mu}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i^{1-\varepsilon}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}}\]

Higher \(\varepsilon\) values penalise inequalities at the bottom more heavily. Unlike the Gini, the Atkinson index integrates an explicit value judgement: it forces the analyst to declare how much they care about inequality at the low end relative to the high end. This makes it more honest than the ostensibly neutral Gini, which implicitly weights all parts of the distribution equally.

The cruel paradox: being poor costs more

Khadija buys her sugar by the 100-gram sachet at the neighbourhood hanout because she never has enough cash in advance to buy a 2-kilo bag. Per gram, she pays significantly more than the affluent family buying in bulk at the hypermarket. The same applies to cigarettes purchased individually, phone credits in small recharges, and water by the bucket where piped connections are absent. This is the “poverty premium”: the less money one has in advance, the more each unit costs. The rich pay less per unit because they can buy in bulk, wait for sales, and negotiate. The poor pay more because they live day to day. Inequality is therefore not only a question of income but of purchasing power per dirham, and the mechanisms that produce inequality in income also produce inequality in effective consumption, compounding the initial disadvantage.

A critical distinction that aggregate Gini figures typically obscure is the difference between income inequality and wealth inequality. Income flows, wages, profits, rents received each year, are unequally distributed. Wealth stocks, the accumulated assets (real estate, financial portfolios, business ownership) that generate those flows, are far more unequally distributed. Globally, the top 1% by wealth typically hold 30-40% of total wealth, while the top 1% by income holds 10-15% of total income. In Morocco, direct wealth distribution data are scarce, but proxies suggest extreme concentration: the top 5% of households hold the majority of real estate and financial assets, while the bottom 50% hold minimal real assets.

Territorial and non-monetary inequality

National Gini figures conceal profound territorial disparities. Rural-urban income gaps in Morocco remain substantial: the HCP estimates that urban households on average spend approximately 60% more than rural households, with the gap wider in consumption of services and processed goods. More important than average gaps are the extremes: the Atlantic coastal metropolitan corridor (Casablanca, Rabat, Tanger, Agadir) has human development indicators approaching those of advanced economies, while interior rural provinces and parts of the mountainous South display indicators comparable to Sub-Saharan Africa.

Non-monetary inequalities often reflect and reinforce income inequalities. The education gap: dropout rates remain high in secondary school, particularly in rural areas. Private schools, representing 10-12% of primary and secondary enrolments but overrepresented in affluent urban families, produce significantly better examination results than public schools, transmitting educational advantage to the children of the already-advantaged. The health gap: infant mortality is approximately twice as high in rural as in urban areas; specialist care and reference hospitals are concentrated in major agglomerations. A patient with a cardiac condition in Midelt does not have the same access to care as one in Casablanca, even nominally covered by the same AMO system. The gender gap: female labour force participation at approximately 19-21% is among the lowest in the world at Morocco’s income level, reflecting simultaneously cultural barriers, insufficient childcare provision, and labour market discrimination that systematically undervalues women’s qualifications.

Part II — The Structural Sources of Moroccan Inequalities

Piketty’s r > g and the structural tendency toward wealth concentration

Thomas Piketty’s Capital in the Twenty-First Century (2014) made a foundational analytical contribution: the long-run tendency of capitalism is toward wealth concentration, and this tendency is not self-correcting. His central result: when the rate of return on capital \(r\) persistently exceeds the economy’s growth rate \(g\), the wealth-to-income ratio rises over time, concentrating wealth in the hands of those who already hold capital.

\[\text{If } r > g \text{ persistently} \Rightarrow \text{wealth share of capital owners rises over generations}\]

The mechanism: capital owners receive returns that exceed what the economy produces for labour; they save and reinvest a fraction of those returns; their wealth grows faster than total income; their share of total wealth rises. This tendency toward concentration is reversed only by exceptional circumstances, wars that destroy accumulated wealth, economic crises that deflate asset values, or deliberate institutional intervention (progressive wealth taxation, inheritance taxes, redistributive social spending).

For Morocco, the r > g dynamic operates through several channels. Real estate has appreciated substantially faster than wages or GDP per capita over the past two decades, particularly in the major urban agglomerations. Those who held real estate wealth saw their relative position improve; those who did not, the majority of the population, particularly rural and informal workers, fell behind in relative terms even as their absolute consumption improved. The absence of meaningful wealth taxation (no general wealth tax, inheritance taxed at low rates, real estate capital gains substantially exempted for primary residences) means that this concentration is not corrected by the fiscal system.

The colonial structural legacy

Morocco’s current economic structure bears the mark of the colonial period (1912-1956). The French Protectorate deliberately developed a dual economy: a modern sector (irrigated commercial agriculture in the Gharb, industrial processing in the major cities, modern commerce) for European settlers and the export economy, and a traditional sector for the Moroccan population, integrated as a reservoir of cheap labour. This initial duality conditioned the distribution of productive assets at independence.

Unlike South Korea and Taiwan, where radical land reforms in the 1950s redistributed land ownership and created a broad base of small property owners, generating the asset base for later industrialisation, Morocco conducted no significant redistributive land reform. European colonial landholdings were “Moroccanised” in 1973, but they primarily benefited families connected to state power. Smallholder farmers did not receive redistributive land allocations, reproducing land concentration under different owners.

This structural starting condition creates persistent intergenerational inequality transmission: the children of disadvantaged rural areas in 1956 transmitted that disadvantage to their children, in a movement of intergenerational persistence that breaks only through massive targeted investment in human capital in lagging areas. The rural-urban divide in educational quality, health access, infrastructure, and economic opportunity is not a recent creation, it is the accumulated result of seventy years of investment patterns that initially reproduced colonial geographic concentration.

Market rents and the political economy of concentration

In economies where certain markets are weakly competitive and entry barriers are high, well-connected actors can capture rents, revenues above their marginal productivity through market position or access to public resources. Morocco’s rent-generating mechanisms are documented and operate through several channels.

Concentrated markets: in cement, food distribution, banking, and telecoms, oligopolistic market structures allow firms to set margins well above competitive levels. The Kaleckian markup relationship (\(P = (1+m) \times CUV\), where \(m\) depends on the degree of monopoly) makes this explicit: concentrated markets generate higher markups, transferring income from consumers and downstream firms to the concentrated sector’s owners.

The taxi owner sleeps while the driver works

At dawn, Brahim takes the wheel of a small taxi that does not belong to him. At the end of the day, he returns the car and 250 dirhams to the licence owner, who has not driven a single minute. The owner holds the right (the taxi licence, often obtained through connections, sometimes inherited, sometimes sold at high prices); Brahim has only his arms and his hours. Who accumulates? The one who holds the title, not the one who does the work. Here in miniature is the heart of wealth inequality: income from capital (owning) grows faster than income from labour (doing). As long as the licence is worth more than the steering wheel, Brahim can work as hard as he likes, he will remain among those who return the 250 dirhams, never among those who pocket them.

Public markets and concessions: procurement procedures of variable transparency, regulations that favour incumbents in energy, transport, and infrastructure concessions, generate rents for the connected rather than value for the state. The rent-seeking literature (Krueger 1974, Tullock 1967) formalises this: economic actors invest resources (time, lobbying, relationship-building) to obtain and maintain regulatory rents rather than create productive value. These investments are socially sterile, they redistribute existing wealth without creating new.

Capital-friendly taxation: the absence of a general wealth tax, low succession taxes for direct heirs (2.5%), substantial exemptions on real estate capital gains, and preferential dividend taxation rates all amplify wealth concentration by allowing capital to accumulate across generations without significant public redistribution.

The analysis of Moroccan rent capitalism, documented by Mohamed Saadi’s comparative work, shows that political connection remains a significant determinant of business success in several sectors. This is not the full extractive-institution pathology of the worst cases; Morocco has competitive elections, a functioning judiciary, and broadly respected commercial law. But the distance from fully inclusive institutions is real and measurable in the gap between Morocco’s economic complexity and what its income level would predict.

Labour market segmentation and gender exclusion

The formal-informal segmentation of Morocco’s labour market directly generates income inequalities. Formal sector workers in large enterprises have wages above the SMIG floor, social insurance (CNSS), access to formal credit, and career progression prospects. Informal workers, representing approximately 40% of non-agricultural employment, receive lower wages with no social protection, no collective rights, and no formal employment history. The income gap between formal and equivalent-qualification informal workers is estimated at 40-60%.

Segmentation is also gendered. Women are overrepresented in informal agricultural labour and domestic work: the two sectors with the weakest social protection. The female labour force participation rate of approximately 19-21% is structurally low, reflecting simultaneously cultural barriers, insufficient childcare infrastructure, transport and security constraints in rural areas, and labour market discrimination that systematically undervalues women’s qualifications equivalent to men’s. An economy that employs fewer than one in five women in the labour force operates substantially below its productive potential, and no monetary or fiscal stimulus can address this structural gap without complementary institutional and social investment.

The functional distribution: wages, profits, and rents

Beyond the personal distribution of income (how much each individual earns), the functional distribution, how national income is divided between wages, profits, and rents, structures the political economy of inequality. Kalecki’s markup pricing model, developed in Chapter 4, connects market structure directly to the wage share: \(P = (1+m) \times CUV\) implies that the profit share in value added equals \(m/(1+m)\), where \(m\) is the markup determined by market concentration. More concentrated markets generate higher markups, compress the wage share, and transfer income from workers and consumers to capital owners.

International comparisons reveal a significant pattern in Morocco’s functional distribution. The labour share of GDP in Morocco is estimated at approximately 35-40% of value added in the marketed sector, compared to 55-65% in OECD economies and 45-55% in comparable middle-income economies. This compressed wage share is simultaneously a reflection of Morocco’s labour market structure (high informality, weak collective bargaining, labour surplus in the Lewis sense) and a contributing cause of its domestic demand constraint: low wages limit domestic consumption, which limits the returns to domestic market-oriented investment, which perpetuates the low-wage equilibrium.

The distributional implications of structural transformation reinforce this analysis. Moving workers from low-wage informal agriculture to higher-wage formal manufacturing raises the wage share, exactly the mechanism through which East Asian industrialisation reduced inequality in its later phases, after the initial Kuznets-curve rise. Morocco’s stagnant formal manufacturing employment share is therefore simultaneously a structural transformation failure, a growth constraint (Thirlwall’s law), and an inequality-perpetuation mechanism.

International comparisons and Morocco’s trajectory

How does Morocco’s inequality profile compare to relevant reference points? Four comparisons illuminate different dimensions.

Tunisia (Gini ~0.33, Palma ~1.5) has somewhat lower measured inequality than Morocco despite similar income levels, reflecting Tunisia’s stronger formal labour market coverage, more developed social protection system, and less extreme territorial disparities. However, Tunisia’s inequality was a major driver of the 2011 revolution: the fact that aggregate Gini figures appeared moderate concealed extreme regional inequalities (coastal urbanised vs interior rural) and youth unemployment that the aggregate measures did not capture.

South Korea (Gini ~0.31, Palma ~1.2) shows the outcome of successful structural transformation combined with strong social protection: a country that industrialised rapidly, paid wages that rose with productivity, and built a comprehensive social insurance system now has one of the more equal income distributions among upper-middle-income economies. The Korean lesson for Morocco: inequality reduction is a product of structural transformation accompanied by labour market institutions, not a consequence of GDP growth alone.

Brazil (Gini ~0.53, Palma ~2.8) shows the opposite: rapid growth with inequitable distribution. Brazil’s Bolsa Família conditional cash transfer programme, the most extensively studied redistribution programme globally, significantly reduced poverty and child mortality, but it did not resolve the structural sources of Brazilian inequality: concentrated land ownership, racial stratification, and concentrated market structures. Post-distribution social programmes can reduce poverty at the bottom without correcting the mechanisms that concentrate wealth at the top.

Morocco in 2030 could plausibly follow either trajectory depending on how current structural choices play out. The NMD 2021’s diagnosis, that Morocco’s growth model has been insufficiently inclusive, and its proposed agenda, productive diversification, human capital investment, social protection generalisation, map exactly onto the analytical framework this chapter has developed. The question is not whether the analysis is correct but whether the political coalition for implementing it can be assembled against the resistance of those who benefit from the current distribution.

Part III — Redistribution: Instruments, Limits, and the Pre-distribution Concept

The Moroccan fiscal system: partial redistribution

Morocco’s tax structure rests primarily on three instruments: VAT (approximately 40% of fiscal revenues, standard rate 20%), Corporate Tax (progressive from 20% to 35% since 2023), and Personal Income Tax (marginal rates from 10% to 38%). Each has distributional properties that aggregate to a system of limited redistribution.

VAT is regressive: poor households spend a higher share of their income on consumption and therefore pay a higher effective VAT rate as a share of income than wealthy households. VAT exemptions on basic necessities (flour, sugar, essential medicines) partially attenuate this regressivity but do not eliminate it. A government that relies heavily on VAT for its revenues is structurally collecting proportionally more from the poor than from the rich.

Personal income tax is theoretically progressive but structurally narrow: its base is dominated by formal sector salaried workers subject to withholding at source. Informal revenues, agricultural incomes (exempted), and capital incomes (capital gains and dividends benefiting from reduced rates) largely escape IR. The effective tax-to-GDP ratio on labour income is far below headline rates because the tax falls on a fraction of total labour income.

The wealth tax is quasi-absent: no general wealth tax exists, successions are taxed at low rates, real estate capital gains are substantially exempted for primary residences. This structure systematically favours intergenerational wealth accumulation and reproduces initial wealth inequalities.

VAT hits Khadija harder than the developer

Khadija the housekeeper and a wealthy real estate developer buy the same packet of sugar: both pay the same VAT. But for Khadija, this tax represents a far heavier share of her modest income than for the developer. This is the nature of consumption taxes: they levy the same rate on all, therefore taking proportionally more from the poor, who spend everything they earn. By contrast, a progressive income tax that rises with wealth makes those with more contribute more. When a fiscal system relies primarily on VAT and little on progressive income taxes, as is largely the case in Morocco, it redistributes poorly, potentially in reverse. The question “who pays the tax?” is never technical: it is the heart of the social contract.

Social transfers and the 2021-2025 social protection reform

Morocco’s social transfer landscape has been restructured through the 2021-2025 social protection reform, one of the most significant domestic policy changes of the decade. The reform’s ambitions are large: universalising health insurance (AMO) coverage to include informal workers and self-employed persons (previously excluded), expanding family allocations through the RSU, and building the infrastructure for eventual unemployment insurance.

The Registre Social Unifié (RSU) is the administrative infrastructure on which the reform depends. It is a centralised database of household circumstances designed to target assistance to those who need it, replacing the previous approach of universal subsidies (which, through the Caisse de Compensation, disproportionately benefited wealthier households consuming more subsidised goods) with direct targeted transfers. The RSU’s conceptual elegance is real: targeted transfers are more efficient than universal price subsidies. Its implementation challenge is equally real: the administrative capacity to correctly identify vulnerable households, maintain data quality, prevent exclusion errors (eligible households incorrectly excluded) and inclusion errors (ineligible households incorrectly included) is substantial.

The Tayssir programme, conditional cash transfers tied to school attendance in rural disadvantaged areas, has demonstrated documented positive effects: evaluations document a 30-40% reduction in school dropout rates in targeted zones. This is a rare example of a Moroccan redistributive policy that has been rigorously evaluated and shows clear impact.

The expansion of AMO to all workers since 2022 represents a genuine inclusion gain: workers previously excluded from any health insurance now have formal coverage. But formal coverage and effective access are not identical: the quality of public health services varies enormously across the territory, and coverage does not translate into equal access to specialists, diagnostic equipment, or reference hospitals.

Pre-distribution: reforming the structures that generate inequality

Anthony Atkinson’s concept of pre-distribution provides the most important analytical distinction for inequality policy: the difference between redistribution ex post (taxing the inequalities the market produces) and redistribution ex ante or pre-distributive (reforming the market structures that generate those inequalities). Pre-distribution attacks the generative mechanisms rather than their consequences.

For Morocco, this distinction is operationally significant. Three pre-distributive policy domains have larger potential impact on inequality than equivalent spending on targeted social programmes:

Competition policy: dismantling the concentrated market structures in cement, distribution, banking, and telecoms that generate above-competitive profits transfers income from consumers and downstream firms to the concentrated sector’s owners. The Conseil de la Concurrence has the mandate; it needs the investigative resources and enforcement authority.

Labour market formalisation: extending formal employment status, social insurance coverage, minimum wage application, collective bargaining rights, to informal workers reduces the wage premium that formal employment enjoys and broadens social protection without requiring additional fiscal transfers.

Transparent public procurement: reforming the awarding of public contracts to ensure competitive selection rather than connection-based allocation reduces the rents captured by politically connected firms and expands the productive base from which tax revenues can be collected.

Each of these reforms creates losers who are well-organised to resist: the oligopolists who benefit from market concentration, the informal-sector employers who benefit from unprotected labour, the connected firms who benefit from preferential procurement. Understanding this political economy of redistribution, who benefits from existing structures, who has the resources to defend them, is indispensable for designing reform strategies that are technically sound and politically achievable.

What redistribution cannot do alone

Three dimensions of Morocco’s inequality are insufficiently addressed by redistribution instruments, even well-designed ones.

Wealth inequalities are not targeted: the fiscal system’s treatment of capital, low succession taxes, real estate capital gains exemptions, dividend rate reductions, systematically allows wealth to compound across generations. The result is that inheritance, not merit or effort, is increasingly the dominant determinant of economic position in the top quartile of the distribution. A meaningful reform of wealth taxation, bringing Morocco’s effective rates on capital toward the OECD average, would generate revenues for human capital investment while directly limiting intergenerational wealth transmission.

Service quality inequalities compound resource inequalities: the formal AMO coverage rate and school enrolment rate have both improved substantially. But a child in a well-equipped Casablanca private school and a child in an underfunded rural public school are not receiving the same education despite the same formal rights. Equalising service quality, through differentiated resource allocation that directs more resources to underperforming schools and health facilities, requires political will to override the interests of urban constituencies better positioned to defend existing distributions of public service quality.

The informal economy traps its participants: workers in the informal economy cannot build social insurance entitlements, cannot access formal credit, cannot demonstrate formal employment history for future formal employment. The informal sector is not a temporary staging ground before formal employment for most of its participants, it is a persistent trap that reduces lifetime earnings and intergenerationally transmits disadvantage. Formalisation incentives, reducing the costs of formal employment for small firms, simplifying social insurance registration, expanding labour inspectorate capacity, are the most direct tools available.

Bourdieu and non-economic capital: cultural and social reproduction

Pierre Bourdieu’s sociological framework adds a dimension that purely economic measures of inequality systematically undercount: cultural capital (educational credentials, cultural knowledge, communicative competence) and social capital (networks of relationships that provide access to resources, opportunities, and influence).

In Bourdieu’s framework, inequalities are reproduced not only through differential access to economic resources (wealth, income) but through differential access to cultural and social resources that convert into economic advantage. A child raised in a household with books, educated parents, and access to cultural institutions accumulates cultural capital that is not measured in household income surveys but translates into educational success, labour market advantage, and social network access. The educational system appears meritocratic, all children formally have access to public schools, while reproducing existing hierarchies because it rewards precisely the cultural capital that is differentially distributed by class origin.

For Morocco, this analysis is directly relevant to the private-public education gap. The 10-12% of children enrolled in private primary and secondary schools are not merely receiving more resources, they are being socialised into the cultural capital (French language fluency, familiarity with academic conventions, networked relationships with other elite-track families) that provides competitive advantage in access to higher education, employment, and social networks. The formal equality of access to public education obscures the functional inequality of outcomes it produces.

The intergenerational transmission of inequality

Inequality persists across generations to a degree that challenges the liberal meritocratic narrative. The intergenerational income elasticity, the correlation between a parent’s income rank and a child’s adult income rank, is high in Morocco, suggesting low social mobility. Sons of professional fathers have systematically higher probabilities of professional employment than sons of agricultural workers, even after controlling for educational attainment.

The mechanisms of intergenerational transmission are multiple and cumulative. Material transmission: wealthy families invest more in children’s education, provide better nutrition and health care, and live in better-resourced neighbourhoods. Cultural transmission: middle and upper-class families transmit academic cultural capital that enhances educational performance beyond material resources. Network transmission: professional networks are transmitted through family contacts, providing privileged access to internships, employment references, and business opportunities. Inheritance: wealth transfers at death directly reproduce asset inequalities across generations without any pretence of meritocracy.

Morocco’s intergenerational mobility is lower than comparable economies with stronger social protection and public education systems, confirming the theoretical prediction that social protection investments and public service quality equalisation are the primary levers for intergenerational mobility, not growth alone.

The fiscal gap: what Morocco’s redistribution does not yet do

The structural gap between Morocco’s redistribution capacity and what its level of inequality requires has three dimensions.

Revenue adequacy: Morocco’s tax-to-GDP ratio (approximately 22-25%) is below the 28-32% typical of comparable middle-income countries with adequate social protection systems. The financing of the 2021-2025 social protection reform requires either increasing revenues (the preferred path, through broadening the tax base to informal income and capital gains) or reallocating existing expenditures (reducing subsidies and inefficient expenditures). Both paths face political resistance from beneficiaries of the status quo.

Incidence analysis: a programme-by-programme incidence analysis of Morocco’s public expenditures would reveal which groups benefit most from government spending. International experience with such analyses consistently shows that wealthier groups capture disproportionate shares of education and health spending (because they have better access to higher-quality institutions), even when headline coverage rates appear universal. Morocco lacks this systematic programme incidence analysis, which means redistribution cannot be designed efficiently.

The informal sector coverage gap: approximately 40% of Morocco’s non-agricultural workforce operates in the informal sector, largely outside social protection coverage. The 2021-2025 reform extends AMO to self-employed workers and independent professionals, a significant step. But informal employees (those working informally for employers who avoid social insurance contributions) remain largely uncovered. Comprehensive coverage requires enforcement capacity and incentive design that makes formal employment attractive to small employers, a challenge involving both labour inspectorate resources and contribution rate calibration.

These three gaps are not principally analytical challenges; they are political economy challenges. Broadening the tax base toward capital income and informal income creates losers who are organised to resist. Reallocating public expenditures toward disadvantaged populations reduces the advantages of currently favoured constituencies. The technical proposals for addressing Morocco’s redistribution gap are well-understood; the political coalition to implement them is the missing ingredient.

Part IV — The Political Economy of Inequality

Understanding why inequality persists despite broad public preference for greater equality requires analysing the political economy of redistribution: who benefits from existing structures, who has the organisational capacity to resist change, and what coalition conditions would allow reform.

The beneficiaries of Morocco’s rent-generating structures, the oligopolists in concentrated markets, the holders of administrative licences, the connected firms in public procurement, are concentrated, organised, well-resourced, and directly aware of the stakes. The beneficiaries of redistribution, informal workers, rural households, women excluded from the formal labour market, are dispersed, not collectively organised, and often unaware of how specific policy choices affect them. This organisational asymmetry is the structural reason why redistribution tends to be incremental and subject to capture.

Kalecki’s 1943 essay “Political Aspects of Full Employment” identified the parallel mechanism in labour markets: full employment is achievable but capitalists resist it because it erodes the disciplinary function of unemployment. The same logic applies to inequality: reducing rents through competition policy, formalising labour markets, and redistributing wealth through taxation are all technically achievable, but each creates organised resistance from those who currently capture the rents.

Successful redistributive reform requires building the political coalition of losers and potential beneficiaries: the consumers who pay above-competitive prices in concentrated markets, the small enterprises that lose public contracts to connected competitors, the formal workers whose social insurance contributions cross-subsidise informal competitors. Making these distributed costs visible and attributing them to identifiable structural causes is the political economy prerequisite for building reform coalitions.

Inequality and macroeconomic performance

The relationship between inequality and economic growth is not a purely distributional question, it has macroeconomic dimensions that connect this chapter to the preceding analysis of aggregate demand, structural transformation, and external constraints.

Inequality compresses domestic demand. A society where income is concentrated in a small wealthy group that saves a high proportion generates less domestic consumption demand per unit of GDP than one where income is distributed more broadly to households with higher marginal propensities to consume. This is the Kaleckian point: wage compression that raises the profit share may increase firms’ notional profits but reduces the domestic market for their output. For Morocco, where domestic demand is a binding constraint on non-export manufacturing investment, reducing inequality is also an expansion of the domestic market.

Inequality and political instability. The economic literature on inequality and growth increasingly finds that high inequality tends to reduce long-run growth rates, not primarily through macroeconomic mechanisms but through political ones: high inequality increases political instability risk, reduces policy continuity, and generates redistributive conflicts that consume resources and attention. Morocco’s 2011 political moment, in which economic discontent (unequal distribution of growth gains, youth unemployment, regional disparities) combined with political aspirations to generate significant reform pressure, illustrates this mechanism directly.

Inequality and the social contract. At its deepest level, the persistence of high inequality in the context of real economic growth represents a failure of the social contract: citizens who see growth occurring but do not share in its gains eventually withdraw consent from the institutional arrangements that sustain that growth. The NMD 2021’s most politically significant contribution was not its specific policy proposals but its institutional acknowledgment, at the highest level of Moroccan governance, that the existing model’s distributional outcomes were unsustainable.

Inequality, trust, and the social contract

The economic literature increasingly documents that high income and wealth inequality erodes social trust, the generalised belief that other citizens are honest and cooperative, in ways that impose real economic costs. Low-trust societies generate higher transaction costs (more legal enforcement, more security expenditure, more insurance), lower public goods provision (free-rider problems are more severe when trust is low), and weaker collective action for common interests including investment in shared infrastructure.

Morocco’s Afrobarometer surveys document relatively high levels of distrust in institutions and between economic actors, particularly across class and regional lines. This institutional low-trust environment is simultaneously a consequence of inequality (economic stratification reduces the sense of shared fate) and a cause of under-provision of the public goods that would reduce inequality. Breaking this low-trust, low-public-goods, high-inequality equilibrium requires simultaneous progress on multiple dimensions: visible redistribution that signals the state is on the side of the disadvantaged, transparent governance that reduces the rent-extraction perception, and quality public services that demonstrate the value of collective provision.

The social protection reform of 2021-2025 is the most important single initiative for addressing this low-trust equilibrium: if it succeeds in providing universal quality health coverage and family support that citizens experience as genuinely equal regardless of their economic position, it will have done more for social trust, and through trust, for economic performance, than any fiscal measure alone.

Conclusion: Distribute to Grow

Morocco’s inequalities are not a fatality: they are the product of identifiable and partially correctable mechanisms. Market concentration generates rents that competition policy can reduce. Wealth inequalities are amplified by capital-friendly taxation that fiscal reform could correct. Educational and health access inequalities are the product of decades of underinvestment in disadvantaged areas that differentiated public resource allocation could partially reverse.

The necessary structural reform combines several levers: more progressive wealth taxation, more active competition policy in concentrated markets, differentiated public investment favouring disadvantaged regions and groups, and construction of a social protection system that is universal in both coverage and quality. These levers are not contradictory with growth; they are its condition in an economy whose dynamic is constrained by the narrowness of domestic demand and the underutilisation of its human capital.

The distributional question is ultimately a question about what kind of society Morocco chooses to be. An economy that generates 4-5% annual growth but concentrates the gains in a narrow segment while 40% of workers remain in informal precarity is not developing inclusively. The NMD 2021’s most important political contribution was making this explicit at the highest governance level: not that inequality exists, but that it is structurally reproduced and that its reproduction is a choice, not a fate. Reversing that choice requires not only technical reforms but the political coalition-building to implement them against the organised resistance of those who benefit from the status quo. Economics identifies the levers; political economy assesses which are available to pull.

What this chapter should leave with the reader

Inequality is multidimensional: income, wealth, opportunity, territorial, and non-monetary inequalities are related but distinct, requiring different measurement tools and policy responses.

The Gini coefficient is a starting point. The Palma ratio (top 10% / bottom 40%), Atkinson index (incorporating explicit normative parameters), and wealth distribution data all reveal dimensions that Gini obscures.

Piketty’s r > g describes the structural tendency toward wealth concentration when capital returns exceed growth. Reversing this tendency requires institutions: progressive wealth taxation, inheritance reform, universal quality public services.

Morocco’s inequalities have identifiable structural sources: colonial legacy, market rents (concentrated markets, administrative licences, public procurement), labour market segmentation (formal-informal wage premium), gendered exclusion, and unequal access to credit.

Pre-distribution (reforming rent-generating market structures) is more efficient than redistribution ex post (taxing resulting inequalities). Competition policy, labour formalisation, and transparent public procurement are Morocco’s most powerful redistributive instruments.

The 2021-2025 social protection reform, AMO generalisation, RSU, family allocations, is the decade’s most significant domestic policy change. Its effectiveness depends on RSU administrative quality, fiscal adequacy, and service quality equalisation.

The political economy of redistribution: inequality persists because its beneficiaries are concentrated and organised while its victims are dispersed. Reform coalitions must make distributed costs visible and attributable.
