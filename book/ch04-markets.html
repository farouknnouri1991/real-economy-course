6  Chapter 4 — Markets and Market Structures

Competition, concentration, and the distribution of power

“It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest.” — Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776)

“People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public.” — Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776)

The guiding thread of this chapter
  1. Exchange creates value before anything is produced. Two people who assign different values to the same good both gain from trading. This is the static foundation of all market economies.
  2. The price mechanism is a coordination device without a coordinator. Under competitive conditions, it allocates resources efficiently. The welfare theorems formalise what that means, and their conditions expose exactly where real markets fall short.
  3. Market structure determines who captures the gains. The price at which exchange occurs reflects not just costs and preferences but power relations: between buyers and sellers, between incumbents and potential entrants, between employers and workers.
  4. Kalecki’s correction is indispensable. In industrial markets, prices are not set by the intersection of supply and demand. They are set by a markup over costs, and the markup depends on the degree of monopoly. Market concentration is directly a mechanism of income distribution.
  5. Digital markets push market power to new extremes. Network effects, two-sided platforms, and data accumulation create winner-take-all dynamics that standard analytical tools were not built to handle.

The Map

Chapter 3 opened the black box of the firm and examined its internal logic. This chapter moves to the arena where firms meet each other and their customers: the market. But the “market” of economic textbooks is not one thing. It is a spectrum from the perfectly competitive souk (twenty sellers of identical tomatoes, one step from a cheaper neighbour) to the monopoly, where the customer has nowhere else to go. Between these extremes lies most of economic life: oligopolies, monopolistically competitive industries, two-sided platforms, and labour markets that are markets of a peculiar and consequential kind.

The chapter advances in five movements. First, why markets exist: the gains of exchange, both static and dynamic. Second, how markets work: supply, demand, price, and the surplus they generate. Third, the Kaleckian correction to the standard price-formation story, which is the single most important heterodox contribution to microeconomics and the one most systematically omitted from introductory courses. Fourth, the spectrum of market structures, from monopoly through oligopoly and its strategic logic, with the Moroccan institutional texture throughout. Fifth, digital markets, where the logic of network effects and two-sided platforms creates a qualitatively new geography of power.

Part I — Why Markets Exist: The Gains of Exchange

Exchange predates markets. Before prices, exchanges, or contracts existed, human beings bartered. Economics begins by understanding why this exchange is mutually beneficial, and under what conditions it may not be.

The pure gain: two students, one cafeteria

Consider a thought experiment. Two students in a university cafeteria: one has a tuna sandwich she dislikes, the other a bottle of water he does not need immediately. They trade. Neither has produced anything new. Yet both are better off than before. This is the pure gain from exchange: the fact that individuals assign different values to the same goods, and voluntary exchange permits a mutually advantageous reallocation.

Economists formalise this through the Edgeworth box: a geometric device where two agents with different initial endowments and different preferences find mutually preferred allocations by exchanging. But Morocco’s joutiya, the informal second-hand market found in every Moroccan city, illustrates it just as well. A leather jacket forgotten in a Casablanca wardrobe finds a buyer in Fès who values it exactly for what it is. No fixed capital, no factory, no technology. Just the diversity of preferences and a place to meet.

The gains from exchange arise from three distinct mechanisms. Preference differences: I like what you do not, and trade makes both of us better off. Endowment differences: I have what you lack, and specialisation creates joint gains. Comparative advantage differences: even if one party is more efficient at everything, specialisation according to relative cost advantage creates gains for both, the logic Ricardo identified in 1817 and that Chapter 14 develops fully in the context of international trade.

The baker who wants shoes, and the invention of money

Imagine a world without money. The baker wants shoes. He goes to the cobbler, who does not need bread but needs meat. The baker must find a butcher who wants bread, exchange for meat, return to the cobbler, if he has not changed his mind. Economists call this the “double coincidence of wants”: for barter to work, each party must want exactly what the other offers, at the same moment. This is exhausting, and most mutually beneficial exchanges simply never happen. Money resolves the blockage: the baker sells bread to anyone, pockets the proceeds, and buys shoes whenever he wishes. Markets and money are not a luxury; they unlock exchanges that would otherwise never occur, and their absence is a genuine source of poverty.

Dynamic gains: trade as a learning system

The static gains (reallocation, specialisation, comparative advantage) are only the visible part of the iceberg. Development economists have identified dynamic gains from trade that operate over the long run and are of an entirely different order.

On the import side, the dynamic gains flow through four mechanisms: competitive discipline (import pressure eliminates the least productive firms and forces others to improve, as Pavcnik showed for India after its 2002 liberalisation); technology diffusion (imported machines from Germany or Japan embody knowledge that the importing country would not have developed alone); access to higher-quality inputs (suppliers in Morocco’s Tanger automotive ecosystem import precision parts that allow them to meet world-industry standards); and benchmarking (the food safety and traceability standards imposed by European markets on Moroccan agri-exporters have restructured entire value chains).

On the export side, dynamic gains flow through economies of scale (a larger market makes R&D and brand building profitable: Brazil’s Embraer could never have become a global aeronautics manufacturer on the Brazilian market alone); learning by doing (South Korean shipyards acquired their excellence by building for demanding foreign clients); and value chain insertion (Vietnamese textile factories supplying Zara gradually absorbed logistics, design, and quality control capabilities that the domestic market would never have required).

Trade is therefore not merely an allocation mechanism. It is a learning system. The gains it generates depend on how firms and institutions use the exposure to upgrade. This distinction, between static gains that raise welfare in the short run and dynamic gains that transform productive capabilities over the long run, is the central analytical contribution of the structuralist tradition in development economics, explored further in Chapters 11 and 12.

Negotiating power and the distribution of gains

One point that standard textbooks pass over too quickly: the price at which exchange occurs is not determined solely by supply and demand. It depends also on the balance of power between the parties.

Consider a small farmer in the Souss region selling his tomatoes to a single collector. Technically, any price between the production cost (say, 1.50 dh/kg) and the wholesale market price (3 dh/kg) represents a mutually advantageous exchange. But who obtains 1.60 dh and who obtains 2.80 dh? The balance of power: the collector has other suppliers; the farmer has no other buyer. If the farmers organise into a cooperative, they regain negotiating capacity. The same reasoning applies.

This is not a special case. It is the general rule. On any market, price reflects both the efficiency of allocation and the equilibrium of power. This is true between an artisan and a tourist in the lanes of the medina (where the pottery price is entirely negotiated), between a Moroccan ministry and a cloud software vendor (a bilateral monopoly with no public price), and between a supermarket and its agricultural suppliers (where large-scale retail often imposes its terms). The structure of the market, the number of actors, their relative size, their ability to exit, determines who captures the larger share of the gains from exchange.

This is precisely why the study of market structures, and not merely “the market” as an abstract entity, is indispensable.

Part II — How Markets Work: Supply, Demand, and the Welfare Theorems

Demand, supply, and equilibrium

The demand curve represents, for each price level, the quantity that consumers are willing to buy. Its downward slope rests on two complementary effects: the substitution effect (a more expensive good pushes consumers toward cheaper substitutes) and the income effect (a more expensive good reduces real purchasing power, reducing consumption of all goods). A single concept underlies both: diminishing marginal utility. The first unit of a good consumed in a day brings more satisfaction than the tenth. The consumer’s reservation price, the maximum she is willing to pay for one additional unit, decreases with quantity consumed. This is why the demand curve slopes down.

The supply curve represents, for each price level, the quantity that firms are willing to produce. Its upward slope rests on rising marginal costs: as output expands, the additional cost of producing one more unit typically increases due to diminishing returns to variable factors. The firm supplies an additional unit only if the price covers the marginal cost of producing it, so higher quantities require higher prices. Market supply is the sum of individual firms’ supplies.

Consumer and producer surplus

One of the most important and least intuitive ideas in microeconomics: the market creates value even when nothing new is produced, simply by enabling each party to buy or sell at a price different from their reservation value.

The consumer surplus is the difference between what a consumer would have been willing to pay and what she actually pays. If you were prepared to pay 150 dirhams for a book and find it for 80, you pocket 70 dirhams of surplus requiring no additional production. The producer surplus is the difference between the price received and the marginal cost of each unit produced. Note the important distinction from profit: producer surplus is measured relative to marginal cost; profit is measured relative to total cost including fixed costs. A new Moroccan industrial firm still recovering its capital investment may generate positive producer surplus while reporting an accounting loss. The sum of the two surpluses is the total welfare created by the market in that period: maximising this total is what the welfare theorems demonstrate competitive equilibrium achieves.

Market equilibrium is the price at which quantity supplied equals quantity demanded, the price that “clears” the market, leaving neither unsatisfied demand nor unsold production. At this point, a remarkable property holds: the total surplus is maximised. No reallocation can improve one party’s situation without worsening another’s.

The price of tomatoes after a night of frost

A night of frost destroys part of the tomato harvest. The next morning at the market there are fewer tomatoes. What happens? The price rises, not because someone malicious decided so, but because buyers compete for a reduced quantity. At the higher price, some forgo tomatoes and turn to courgettes; others pay. The market finds on its own a new point where what is offered finds a taker. Nobody orchestrates this: it is the convergence of thousands of individual decisions. This is the beauty, and the coldness, of the price mechanism: it coordinates without a conductor, but it does not ask whether the person who forgoes tomatoes does so by choice or by lack of means.

Elasticity measures the sensitivity of quantity demanded to price changes. The price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in quantity demanded per one percent change in price. When elasticity (in absolute value) exceeds 1, demand is elastic: quantity responds more than proportionally to price. When it is below 1, demand is inelastic: quantity is relatively insensitive. This distinction is not technical decoration. It shapes everything in policy. Taxes on inelastic goods, basic foodstuffs, essential medicines, fall heavily on consumers who cannot reduce their consumption. Taxes on elastic goods, luxury items, non-essential services, spread between producers and consumers, with a larger share borne by whoever faces stiffer competition. For Morocco, the 2022 inflation episode illustrated this dramatically: food prices surged, and households dependent on bread, cooking oil, and sugar, goods with very inelastic demand, bore a disproportionate real income loss.

The two welfare theorems and their conditions

The great intellectual achievement of 20th-century microeconomics was to give a rigorous demonstration of Adam Smith’s intuition. The invisible hand is not a vague metaphor: it is a precise theorem. Under certain conditions, the competitive equilibrium is Pareto optimal, no reallocation can make anyone better off without making someone else worse off.

The competitive equilibrium simultaneously achieves two forms of efficiency. Productive efficiency: competition forces firms to produce at minimum cost; inefficient firms are eliminated. Allocative efficiency: the quantities produced correspond exactly to consumer preferences. The condition P = MC ensures that the marginal value consumers assign to the last unit produced equals the marginal cost of producing it. Neither too much nor too little is produced.

This property is genuinely remarkable: a fully decentralised mechanism, with no central coordinator, no plan, no aggregated information, just millions of agents pursuing their private interest, delivers an optimal allocation. The mathematical formalisation of Smith’s invisible hand by Arrow and Debreu in 1954 is one of the monuments of economic theory.

A further result deserves emphasis: in the long run, competitive markets drive economic profits toward zero. Not accounting profits, but profits above and beyond the normal return on capital. The mechanism is simple: any positive profit attracts entrants, who increase supply, drive down prices, and erode the surplus until no further entry is profitable. This implies the sharp formulation: “if everyone knows how to do it, there is no profit.” Durable profits require a barrier, an informational advantage, a genuine innovation, or a natural resource rent. The implication for Moroccan development is direct: sectors where profits remain structurally elevated over years signal either entry barriers worth examining for their welfare consequences, or genuine scarcity rents (like phosphate extraction) that raise distinct questions about who captures the national patrimony and on what terms.

But the theorem is conditional. The conditions that make it hold are never fully satisfied in any real economy. Perfect information: all agents know the prices and qualities of all goods. Absence of market power: no agent is large enough to influence prices. No externalities: each decision affects only the decision-maker. Free entry and exit: any firm can enter any market without prohibitive cost. Pure private goods: no public goods, no common resources.

These are not approximately true. They are systematically violated. Real markets are full of information asymmetries (who can genuinely evaluate the quality of a drug or a mortgage?), market power (Morocco’s three cement producers are not price-takers), externalities (pollution, vaccines, innovation), and entry barriers. The welfare theorem is a logical demonstration of a property of an ideal world, not a description of the real one. Its value is as a reference point: by showing under what conditions markets work perfectly, it reveals exactly why and how they fail.

Market equilibrium is efficient. It is not equitable. These are different statements.

A Pareto-optimal outcome is one where no further exchange can make anyone better off without making someone worse off. All mutually beneficial trades have occurred. But the cake can be shared very unequally and still be Pareto optimal. If the richest person holds everything and all possible trades have taken place, the allocation is Pareto optimal but deeply unjust. A housing market can be efficient, all profitable transactions are completed, while simultaneously being inaccessible to 40% of the population. These two judgements do not contradict each other; they describe different things. Efficiency says the size of the cake is maximised. Equity asks how it is cut. These require different analytical tools, and the second question is not made less important by answering the first.

Part III — The Kaleckian Correction: How Industrial Prices Are Actually Set

The standard story just presented, price determined by the intersection of supply and demand, adjustment through market clearing, is the dominant representation in textbooks. It is useful. It is not universal. Prices do not form the same way in all sectors, and ignoring this leads to serious diagnostic errors in economic policy.

Michał Kalecki, a Polish economist working in the 1930s-1960s, proposed a luminous distinction between two regimes of price formation according to the flexibility of supply.

Market failures: four structural sources of inefficiency

The welfare theorems show what markets can do under ideal conditions. Real markets fail along four systematic dimensions, each justifying a different form of institutional response.

Information asymmetries arise when one party knows more about the quality of a good than the other. George Akerlof’s 1970 “Market for Lemons” demonstrated that this can destroy entire markets: if buyers cannot distinguish good used cars from defective ones, they offer only an average price; sellers of good cars withdraw; average quality falls; prices fall; and the market may collapse entirely. The same mechanism operates in Morocco’s credit market for SMEs: banks cannot easily distinguish viable from risky projects among small enterprises without credit histories. The result is credit rationing, not from lack of liquidity but from information failure. The creation of Morocco’s credit bureau (CRC) and Tamwilcom’s guarantee mechanisms are institutional responses to this specific market failure.

Externalities occur when a decision imposes costs or confers benefits on third parties not involved in the transaction, without compensation. The intensive irrigation of Morocco’s Souss-Massa basin generates a negative externality on shared aquifers: each farmer pumps from a common water table without internalising the depletion cost imposed on neighbours and future generations. Conversely, Morocco’s agri-exporters who adopt drip irrigation and quality certification generate a positive externality on the entire sector’s reputation in European markets, benefiting competitors who bore none of the investment cost. These two externalities justify, respectively, water use regulation with pricing mechanisms and subsidisation of investment in quality infrastructure.

Public goods are non-rival (one person’s use does not diminish availability for others) and non-excludable (it is impossible to prevent anyone from benefiting). National defence, basic research, macroeconomic stability, and weather forecasting are examples. Because private firms cannot capture returns from non-excludable goods, they are systematically under-provided by markets. The state’s provision of these goods is not a correction of market failure but the fulfilment of a function the market structurally cannot perform.

Market power, the ability of one or a few actors to set prices above marginal cost, generates a deadweight loss: socially beneficial exchanges that would occur under competitive conditions do not take place. This is the subject of the market structure analysis that follows.

Two regimes

In flexible-supply markets, industry, manufactured goods, most services, firms have idle capacity and relatively stable (sometimes constant) marginal costs. They do not lower their prices in the face of falling demand. Instead, they reduce output, put workers on short-time schedules, and manage inventories. Prices are rigid; volumes fluctuate. If you ask a cement producer why she does not cut prices when orders fall, the answer is revealing: she would be interpreted as signalling weakness, triggering retaliatory price cuts from competitors, destroying the price structure, and ending up with lower revenue and lower margins. Better to reduce production and maintain the margin.

In rigid-supply markets, agriculture, raw materials, non-storable goods, adjustment occurs through price. The quantity supplied is fixed in the short run (the harvest is what it is). When demand increases, price rises; when demand falls, price drops. Prices are volatile; quantities are stable.

This distinction is not academic. It is essential for interpreting the 2022 Moroccan inflation episode. The surge in food prices (over 11% according to the HCP) was a rigid-supply phenomenon: international grain and edible oil prices, driven by Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and post-COVID supply constraints, transmitted directly into consumer prices because domestic agricultural output could not quickly expand. The appropriate response, targeted subsidies on basic foods and emergency social transfers, was precisely calibrated to a supply-shock model, not a demand-pull model. Treating the two as equivalent would have produced the wrong diagnosis and the wrong policy.

The markup rule

In industrial markets, Kalecki formalises a pricing rule that can be written simply:

\[P = (1 + m) \cdot CUV\]

where \(P\) is the selling price, \(CUV\) the unit variable cost (raw materials plus direct wages), and \(m\) the markup rate, the margin applied by the enterprise.

This markup is not arbitrary. It depends on the degree of monopoly in the sector: the weaker the competition, the higher the markup firms can sustain. In a highly concentrated sector (cement, telecoms, mass retail), \(m\) is higher than in a competitive sector (market gardening, small-scale tailoring). This is Kalecki’s first direct link between market structure and price formation: the markup is an institutional variable, not merely a technical one.

The macroeconomic implications follow directly. If firm revenue is divided between wages \(W\) and profits \(\Pi\), one can show that:

\[\frac{\Pi}{Y} = \frac{m}{1+m}\]

The profit share in value added is an increasing function of the markup, and therefore of the degree of monopoly. A more concentrated sector mechanically transfers a larger share of value added to capital owners and a smaller share to workers. Market concentration is directly a mechanism of income distribution. This result links market structure to inequality in a way that the supply-demand framework simply cannot see.

Markup pricing and the wage share in Morocco

World Bank and HCP data suggest that the wage share in Moroccan value added is structurally low, around 35-40% according to HCP national accounts, against 50-55% in comparable Asian economies and 55-65% in European developed economies. Kaleckian theory provides a direct explanation: in the concentrated sectors that dominate Morocco’s formal economy (cement, telecoms, large-scale retail, banking), high markups compress the wage share. When Lafarge, Holcim, and Ciments du Maroc set prices in a segmented regional market where transport costs protect each producer from distant competition, and when the telecom duopoly between Maroc Telecom and Orange sets tariffs on a market where entry is blocked by spectrum licensing, the markup reflects not productive efficiency but market power. Oligopolistic concentration is therefore not merely a problem for consumer prices; it is a mechanism for the distribution of income between capital and labour.

The macroeconomic consequence

The post-Keynesian analysis extends Kalecki’s point to the business cycle with a conclusion that overturns a great deal of conventional wisdom. When an industrial firm faces a demand downturn, it does not generally cut prices. Cutting prices would be interpreted by competitors as a weakness signal, triggering destructive price wars. The firm prefers to reduce output and maintain margins. This is adjustment through quantities, not prices.

The macroeconomic consequence is profound: industrial recessions do not resolve themselves through spontaneous price deflation but through declining employment and income, until demand recovers. This is why fiscal stimulus during recessions, the subject of Chapter 9, can increase output without immediately triggering inflation: in the face of idle industrial capacity, producers expand output at roughly constant prices rather than raising them. The Keynesian diagnosis of effective demand shortfalls, and the Kaleckian diagnosis of their distributional consequences, are two sides of the same analytical coin.

Part IV — Market Structures: A Geography of Power

The real economy is not a single market. It is a geography of market power: configurations that differ by the number of actors, their relative size, the nature of products, and especially the barriers that prevent or slow the entry of new competitors.

The spectrum from competition to monopoly

The Tuesday souk and the only water supplier

At the weekly souk, twenty merchants sell identical tomatoes side by side. Nobody can cheat on price: if one asks two dirhams more, the customer takes three steps and buys from a neighbour. Prices are shouted, known to all, aligned. No seller “sets” the price, the market sets it for all of them. This is the closest real approximation to the “perfect competition” of textbooks: many sellers, identical product, transparent information, free entry. Now compare this with the only large supermarket in a small town, or the sole water distributor. There, the dissatisfied customer cannot take three steps to a competitor. Between the Tuesday souk and monopoly, the entire economics of market structures lies in this question: can the unhappy customer take three steps elsewhere?

The monopoly: one master facing the market

The monopoly is the simplest market structure to analyse and the most socially costly. A single supplier faces captive demand. Because the monopolist faces the entire market demand, choosing output also determines price. To sell one additional unit, the monopolist must lower the price not only on that unit but on all units already sold. The marginal revenue is therefore less than price, and this asymmetry is the source of all the monopolist’s inefficiencies.

The monopolist maximises profit by producing the quantity where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. The price is then read off the demand curve at that quantity, always above marginal cost, unlike the competitive firm where P = MC.

With linear demand \(P(Q) = a - bQ\) and constant marginal cost \(c\):

\[Q^M = \frac{a-c}{2b}, \qquad P^M = \frac{a+c}{2}\]

Compare with perfect competition: \(Q^{CP} = \frac{a-c}{b}\) and \(P^{CP} = c\). The monopolist produces half the competitive quantity and sells at a higher price. This is not a nuance; it is a massive difference. The gap generates a deadweight loss, socially beneficial exchanges that do not occur because consumers willing to pay more than the marginal cost of production are not served. This lost value benefits no one; it is pure waste.

The Lerner index \(L = (P - MC)/P\) measures market power. In perfect competition, \(L = 0\). The deeper relation is \(L = 1/|\varepsilon_D|\): market power depends on the inelasticity of demand. Firms can maintain high markups only where clients cannot easily turn to substitutes. This is why patented medicines, drinking water, and energy are sectors with high potential market power, and why their regulation is a public interest, not merely a matter of economic efficiency.

Not every monopoly is equally harmful. A monopoly born of genuine innovation earns a temporary rent that is the reward, and the incentive, for the innovation. The patent system is precisely a legally granted monopoly: temporary exclusivity as a prize for R&D. A natural monopoly arises when fixed costs are so high and marginal costs so low that the cost structure favours a single actor, electricity transmission, railway infrastructure, water distribution. Duplicating these networks would be a colossal waste. ONEE (the national electricity and water utility) is a natural monopoly on the network. Its progressive block tariff, early kilowatt-hours cheaper, subsequent ones progressively more expensive, is a form of second-degree price discrimination that reconciles universal access with financial viability.

Oligopoly, strategic interdependence, and game theory

An oligopoly is not simply a market with few firms. It is a situation of strategic interdependence: a firm’s pricing or output decision depends on what it anticipates rivals will do, and vice versa. This mutual anticipation is what makes oligopolistic analysis fundamentally different from monopoly analysis, and it is why economists developed game theory.

Game theory was born formally in 1944 with von Neumann and Morgenstern’s Theory of Games and Economic Behavior, then given its central equilibrium concept by John Nash in 1950. A Nash equilibrium is a profile of strategies where no player has an incentive to deviate unilaterally, given the strategies of the others. It is not necessarily the collectively optimal outcome; it is the stable outcome where no one has regret about their choice in retrospect.

The three canonical models of oligopoly each illuminate a different dimension.

Cournot competition (simultaneous quantity choice) produces an equilibrium between the monopoly and the competitive outcome. Each firm treats the rival’s quantity as given and chooses its best response. The Nash equilibrium is where these best-response functions intersect: positive profits, prices above marginal cost, but more output and lower prices than the monopoly. This model fits markets like cement, oil, and bulk chemicals, where firms commit to production capacity before knowing rivals’ choices.

Bertrand competition (simultaneous price choice with homogeneous products) produces a paradoxical result: two firms are sufficient for prices to fall to marginal cost, the competitive outcome. The logic cuts sharply: if two firms sell identical goods and both are above marginal cost, any firm can capture the entire market by undercutting the rival by a tiny amount. The rival responds by undercutting again. The race to the bottom continues until neither can profitably cut further. The logic is sharp: if Firm A sets a price above marginal cost, Firm B undercuts by a tiny amount, captures the entire market, and earns nearly all the profits. Firm A responds by undercutting again. The undercutting cascade continues until P = MC. This is the Bertrand paradox: duopoly achieves the competitive outcome. The resolution: in practice, products are differentiated, capacity constraints bind, or firms compete in repeated interactions where cooperation can be sustained.

Stackelberg competition (sequential quantity choice) gives the first-mover a structural advantage. The leader commits to a quantity, the follower observes and plays its best response. The leader, anticipating the follower’s reaction function, produces more than the Cournot level, leaving the follower with less. This model fits markets where an incumbent has committed to capacity before a challenger enters.

The prisoner’s dilemma in the Moroccan cement market

Morocco’s cement market is served by a small number of producers, Lafarge Maroc, Ciments du Maroc, and Asment, whose regional transport cost structures naturally segment the national market. Each producer faces a classic prisoner’s dilemma: if all limit production and maintain high prices, all earn healthy margins. But each is individually tempted to increase output slightly to capture additional market share. If one defects, the others must respond to avoid losing volume. The equilibrium tends toward excess capacity: each producer maintains more capacity than needed, both as insurance against demand peaks and as a credible signal that it will respond aggressively to any attempt at market entry. The Conseil de la Concurrence examined this sector in 2020, finding patterns of parallel pricing that are at least consistent with tacit coordination, though proving explicit collusion remains difficult when identical prices can also be explained by firms facing identical costs and responding rationally to the same market signals.

Entry barriers: why concentration persists

The central question is not the number of firms currently active but the barriers that prevent new entry. Without barriers, profits attract entrants, competition intensifies, and profits erode. With barriers, profits persist.

Barriers arise from multiple sources. Structural economies of scale make large-scale production cheaper per unit: a cement kiln must operate at minimum efficient scale to be competitive, and that scale is large relative to regional demand. Network effects in digital markets make entry progressively harder as the incumbent accumulates users whose presence attracts more users. Switching costs lock in existing customers: a mobile operator whose subscribers have built up contact lists, payment histories, and loyalty points faces less competitive pressure than a market without these frictions. Sunk costs, investments in brand, technology, or regulatory approvals that cannot be recovered on exit, deter entry because they represent irrecoverable commitments. Regulatory barriers confer protected positions through licensing, concessions, and spectrum allocation; in Morocco, banking licences, telecom spectrum, and electricity production permits are examples.

Incumbent firms also create barriers strategically. Predatory pricing involves accepting temporary losses to eliminate an existing competitor, then raising prices once the rival has exited, illegal in most jurisdictions but difficult to prove. Limit pricing involves maintaining prices sufficiently low to make entry unattractive, sacrificing short-run profits to protect market position. Strategic excess capacity is a commitment to respond aggressively to entry: a cement producer maintaining half a million tonnes of idle capacity signals credibly that it can flood the market if a new entrant appears, making the investment in entry unattractive before it is even made.

Maroc Telecom: strategic pricing and brand moats

When Inwi entered the Moroccan telecom market in 2010, a price war erupted that reduced tariffs by 30-50% on some segments. Maroc Telecom (IAM) responded by targeting price reductions precisely on the segments most contested, while maintaining margins on captive business clients and rural zones where its network coverage was unmatched. This selective price response is textbook limit pricing: accept lower margins where competition is real, protect margins where the competitor cannot follow. The subsequent decade has seen prices stabilise in a zone of competitive oligopoly, with observable parallel pricing that the ANRT monitors but cannot easily attribute to explicit coordination rather than rational parallel response to common market conditions.

Monopolistic competition: many sellers, differentiated products

Between perfect competition and oligopoly sits a structure that describes much of retail trade, restaurants, hotels, personal services, and a large share of manufacturing: monopolistic competition. Many firms, differentiated products, and relatively free entry. Each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve (its product is not a perfect substitute for rivals’) but cannot sustain positive profits over the long run because free entry erodes them.

Joan Robinson’s The Economics of Imperfect Competition (1933) was the foundational analysis of this structure. Robinson showed that any market where firms face downward-sloping demand, that is, virtually all real markets, involves pricing above marginal cost, a departure from competitive efficiency. She went further: the degree of monopoly embedded in any market structure is directly reflected in the functional distribution of income. A higher markup means a higher profit share in value added and a lower wage share. Robinson’s analysis was, among other things, a theory of why capitalism tends to produce inequality, and why the structure of product markets is inseparable from the structure of income distribution.

Robinson wrote in dialogue with Kalecki, and the two analyses are complementary: Kalecki provides the dynamic theory of price formation (the markup rule and its macroeconomic consequences), while Robinson provides the static welfare analysis (the departure from marginal cost and the distributional implications of that departure). Together, they make the case that the standard competitive model is not a description of typical market outcomes but of an extreme ideal that is rarely approached.

The short-run equilibrium of monopolistic competition resembles monopoly: P > MC, positive profits. But free entry erodes those profits until the typical firm earns zero economic profit in the long run, with P = AC > MC. Resources are not used at minimum average cost, there is excess capacity, but consumers benefit from variety. Morocco’s urban service economy (cafés, hair salons, pharmacies, restaurants, small clothing retailers) broadly fits this structure: many firms, meaningful differentiation, relatively easy entry, thin margins, and persistent overcapacity in the retail space that reflects the absence of a binding minimum viable scale. The trade-off between productive efficiency and product variety is the central tension of this structure, and the Moroccan version adds a twist: the informal sector reduces effective entry barriers further, creating a competitive fringe that limits formal firms’ pricing power even more than their number alone would suggest.

Part V — Digital Markets: Network Effects, Platforms, and Surveillance Capitalism

Digital markets are not simply markets with digital products. They are markets where the structural assumptions of the previous analysis, declining marginal costs, independent consumer preferences, price as the central coordination signal, are systematically violated. Understanding how requires a set of analytical concepts that did not exist in 1930.

Network effects and winner-take-all dynamics

An isolated telephone is useless. Two telephones enable a conversation. A million telephones create a social infrastructure. The value of the telephone does not come from its intrinsic characteristics: it comes from the number of people who possess one. This is the network effect in its purest form.

Robert Metcalfe observed that the value of a communications network grows proportionally to the square of the number of its users. If a network has \(n\) users, the number of possible connections is \(n(n-1)/2\), which grows like \(n^2\). The value grows quadratically while the cost of connecting an additional user is roughly constant. This gap between linear cost and quadratic value is the source of the increasing returns to adoption that characterise network markets.

Why everyone stays on WhatsApp

You find that a competing messaging application is more secure, more beautiful, faster. You install it. Problem: you are alone. Your mother, your colleagues, the family group, the building syndic, the delivery driver, all are on WhatsApp. The most “practical” application is not the technically best one: it is the one where the others are already present. This is the network effect. Its consequence is formidable: even if a superior application appears, nobody migrates alone, because migrating alone has no benefit. It would require everyone to move simultaneously, and nobody goes first. This is why digital giants, once established, become almost impossible to dislodge: they do not sell a product, they sell the presence of everyone else.

Markets with strong network effects tend toward monopoly or tight oligopoly. A platform with more users offers more value; users migrate toward the larger platform; the larger platform attracts more users. This self-reinforcing positive feedback loop produces winner-take-all markets. WhatsApp, Google Search, LinkedIn, Amazon Marketplace: in each segment, one or two platforms dominate structurally. The tipping point, the critical user mass above which a platform’s growth becomes self-sustaining, explains why commercial wars between competing platforms are so violent in their initial phases: it is a race to cross the tipping point before rivals do, even at a sustained loss.

Switching costs lock users in once the platform is established. Leaving Facebook means losing one’s contacts. Leaving WhatsApp means persuading one’s entire social network to follow. Leaving the iPhone means reconfiguring applications, habits, and the iMessage ecosystem. These costs transform first-mover advantages into durable entry barriers.

Two-sided platforms and the Tirole-Rochet framework

The digital economy’s most structurally distinctive institution is the two-sided platform: a firm that simultaneously serves two distinct groups of agents whose cross-externalities determine the optimal pricing structure.

Jean Tirole (Nobel Prize 2014) and Jean-Charles Rochet formalised this theory in 2003. Their central contribution: on a two-sided market, the structure of prices between the two sides is determined not by relative costs but by relative elasticities and the intensity of cross-network effects. The rule is: subsidise the side that generates the most value for the other side; monetise the side that generates less value for the first.

The nightclub and the two-sided platform logic

A nightclub often admits women for free while charging men full price. Unjust? No, it is platform economics. The club serves two groups seeking each other out. It subsidises the side that attracts the other. The more women present, the more men come (and pay); so subsidise women to monetise men. Google does exactly the same: it offers free search to internet users (the side that attracts) to charge advertisers (the side that pays). When a service is offered “for free”, always ask: which side of the market am I on? Am I the subsidised bait, or the paying customer?

This logic produces pricing structures that standard competition analysis cannot evaluate with the usual tools. The price of a product set at zero does not imply zero market power, it implies that the platform is monetising the other side of the market. The concentration of Google in search (over 90% of global searches) cannot be assessed merely by asking whether the price to users is above marginal cost, because the price to users is zero. Competition analysis on two-sided markets must account for the full pricing structure across both sides, the intensity of cross-network effects, and the barriers to multi-homing (the ability of users to use multiple platforms simultaneously).

Morocco’s digital economy: platforms in a transitional context

Morocco’s digital economy illustrates the dynamics of network effects and two-sided platforms in an economy where mobile penetration is high (over 50 million SIM cards for a population of 37 million), internet penetration still expanding (above 90% among youth), and fintech infrastructure being actively constructed.

The most consequential platform battle is in mobile payments. CMI (Centre Monétique Interbancaire), M2T (Maroc Telecom’s mobile money service), and new digital banking entrants are each attempting to reach the critical user mass that would trigger self-sustaining network effects. The stakes are enormous: approximately 40% of the adult population remains unbanked, and informal cash transactions represent a massive share of daily commerce. The platform that successfully connects informal merchants and consumers through a simple payment system will acquire a structurally dominant position in Moroccan payments, with very high switching costs once routines are established.

Jumia Morocco illustrates the difficulty of building a two-sided marketplace platform in an emerging economy. To attract buyers, it needs sellers and fast delivery. To attract sellers, it needs buyers. This chicken-and-egg problem requires heavy subsidisation of both sides during the growth phase (delivery subsidies for buyers, reduced commission rates for sellers), which generates sustained losses until critical mass is reached. Jumia’s profitability remained elusive because the subsidies needed to keep both sides engaged exceeded the value it could extract through commissions. This is the structural challenge of two-sided platform building in markets where trust in e-commerce is still being established and logistics infrastructure is relatively expensive.

Surveillance capitalism and the data economy

The dominant business model of the largest digital platforms rests not on selling products to users but on extracting behavioural data and selling predictive profiles to advertisers. Shoshana Zuboff calls this surveillance capitalism: what is being sold is not a product but the prediction and modification of human behaviour.

Data have unusual economic properties that place them in a grey zone between public and private goods. They are non-rival: my use of a data set does not prevent your simultaneous use. They may be non-excludable in production (behavioural data is generated involuntarily by users) but are excludable in ownership, platforms claim proprietary rights over data generated on their infrastructure. These properties create a form of capital that compounds in value with scale in ways physical capital does not: a larger database generates more accurate predictions, which generate more advertising revenue, which fund better algorithms, which attract more users, which generate more data. The competitive advantage of data accumulation is self-reinforcing and cumulative.

For competition analysis, this creates a fundamental problem: the standard test for market power asks whether a firm can profitably raise prices by 5-10%. When the price is zero, as with Google search or Facebook, this test is literally inapplicable. The relevant question is not whether the price exceeds marginal cost but whether users are paying a non-monetary price (their attention, their data, their behavioural modification) that constitutes an economic transfer whose value they neither observe nor negotiate. Shoshana Zuboff’s concept of surveillance capitalism captures this economy’s distinctive character: what is being sold is not a product, but the prediction and modification of human behaviour.

Digital platform power in Morocco: a different vantage point

For Morocco, the economics of surveillance capitalism takes a specific form that the standard Silicon Valley analysis misses. With over 25 million active Facebook and Instagram users, over 15 million on TikTok, and a young population (60% under 35) heavily present on social networks, global attention platforms have captured a significant share of Moroccan daily attention. The Moroccan digital advertising market, roughly 1 billion MAD in 2023, directs more than 70% of digital ad spending to Google and Meta. Moroccan television and the national press suffer structural disintermediation: advertising revenue migrates to platforms whose headquarters, servers, and tax residences are in California or Dublin. This is a form of international digital rent: foreign enterprises extract value (data, attention, time) from the Moroccan population, transform it into profits realised in their headquarters, and reinject it partially as free services to users. Neither current Moroccan tax law nor the data protection framework of the CNDP captures this asymmetry fully. The question of digital sovereignty, developing Moroccan platforms capable of reaching national critical mass, is as much a political economy question as a technology question.

Part VI — The Labour Market: A Market of a Different Kind

The labour market is not a market like the others. What is exchanged is not a commodity but a human capacity. The conditions of exchange determine not merely a price but the daily circumstances of millions of lives. This asymmetry between the product market analysis and the labour market analysis is not a footnote; it is one of the most consequential differences in applied economics.

Why wages do not easily fall

The standard labour market model predicts that in the presence of excess supply (unemployment), wages will fall until equilibrium is restored. Empirically, this prediction is regularly contradicted: wages are rigid downward. Several mechanisms explain this.

Efficiency wages: Akerlof and Yellen showed that firms may rationally pay above the market-clearing wage to reduce turnover (costly to replace), improve productivity (better-paid workers engage more), and retain the best employees (who have more alternatives). If all firms reason this way, the equilibrium wage remains above the level that would clear the market, and unemployment persists at equilibrium.

Fairness norms: workers do not perceive their wage as a simple price. They perceive it as an evaluation of their contribution and their status. A wage cut, even slightly above the equilibrium level, is perceived as an injustice and generates reduced effort (work-to-rule, absenteeism, turnover). Firms anticipate this reaction and avoid nominal wage reductions even during low-demand periods, preferring layoffs.

Rotation costs: hiring (recruitment, training) and firing (severance, procedures) both have real costs. These costs encourage firms to stabilise their workforce rather than adjusting wages continuously to demand fluctuations.

Monopsony and employer power

Standard theory assumes perfect competition on both sides of the labour market. On many local markets, this assumption is false: one or a few employers dominate the labour basin. This is monopsony, the buying-side counterpart of monopoly.

In the presence of monopsony, the dominant employer can set wages below the competitive full-employment level, limiting hiring to push down the equilibrium wage. The consequence is sub-optimal employment and wages below marginal productivity. Card and Krueger’s seminal 1994 study showed empirically that minimum wage increases had not reduced employment in local markets dominated by powerful employers, precisely because the monopsonist was already paying below the competitive level, and the minimum wage was correcting the distortion rather than creating one.

Mining basins and monopsony in Morocco

The phosphate mining basins of Khouribga and Youssoufia, where OCP is the dominant employer, approximate monopsony conditions. OCP workers benefit from wages and conditions significantly above the regional average, but OCP’s subcontractors and local service providers are in a structurally weak negotiating position: their workers know they cannot easily find equivalent employment elsewhere. The presence of a single large employer in a region is therefore not neutral on the wages of the rest of the labour basin: it both provides anchor employment at high wages for the directly employed and exercises downward pressure on wages throughout the supply chain, through the simple mechanism of limited labour demand from alternative employers.

The wage share and distribution: Kalecki applied to labour markets

The Kaleckian markup framework, developed earlier for product markets, has a direct application to labour markets that completes the distributional analysis. If the price is set as P = (1+m) × CUV, and CUV includes direct wage costs, then the wage share in value added is:

\[ rac{W}{VA} = rac{1}{1+m} imes rac{W}{CUV}\]

A higher markup compresses the wage share, holding everything else constant. This means that product market concentration, through its effect on the markup, directly reduces the labour share of national income. This is not a marginal effect. The structural difference between Morocco’s wage share (around 35-40%) and that of comparable developing countries with less concentrated market structures is partly explained by exactly this mechanism.

The macroeconomic consequence is important for understanding Morocco’s growth model. A compressed wage share means household consumption is structurally limited relative to what productivity would otherwise permit. This creates a structural tendency toward insufficient domestic demand that must be compensated by export growth, public investment, or external financing. Morocco’s persistent current account deficit, the economy consistently importing more than it exports, is partly the macroeconomic expression of an income distribution in which the wage share is too low to sustain domestic consumption adequate for productive capacity utilisation.

Labour market segmentation and the graduate unemployment paradox

The labour market is not a single market but a set of relatively sealed segments, separated by barriers to mobility. The dual labour market model (Doeringer and Piore, 1971) distinguishes a primary segment, formal, stable, well-remunerated employment with social protection (CNSS, AMO, paid leave), from a secondary segment, informal, precarious, poorly remunerated work without protection.

Segmentation is stable because primary-segment firms maintain wages above market clearing (efficiency wages) and select candidates through costly signals (diplomas, prior experience in formal employment). Secondary-segment workers cannot simply apply to primary-segment employers without the required signals. The barriers are institutional as much as productive.

Morocco’s graduate unemployment paradox

Labour market segmentation explains a Moroccan paradox that standard models find baffling: the unemployment rate among university graduates (approximately 20-23% in 2023) is significantly higher than the national average (12-13%). This is not because graduates are less productive than non-graduates. It is because graduates refuse secondary-segment employment and wait for primary-segment positions, public sector, large formal enterprise, that match their educational level. This wait is individually rational: accepting a manual handling job with an engineering degree signals low quality to primary-segment employers and may permanently close doors. But collectively, if all graduates wait, insertion delays lengthen for everyone. The solution is not labour market “flexibilisation”, which in practice means lowering primary-segment protections to secondary-segment levels. It is increasing the number of quality formal jobs in the primary segment, through industrial policy and productive public investment that creates employment requiring the skills the educational system produces.

Conclusion: Markets as Economic and Social Institutions

This chapter has established the microeconomic foundations of the course. Four essential acquisitions.

Markets create value when they enable mutually beneficial exchanges: static gains from reallocation, dynamic gains from learning and capability-building. This is real, documentable, and constitutes the main economic justification for market economies.

Markets also fail, through externalities, public goods, information asymmetries, and market power. These failures are not accidents. They are structural properties of certain markets, and they define the legitimate perimeter of public intervention. Chapters 5 and 6 develop the institutional responses.

Market structure determines distribution. Kalecki’s insight, that the markup reflects the degree of monopoly and directly determines the profit share of value added, links market concentration to income inequality in a way the standard supply-demand framework cannot capture. The distribution of the gains from exchange is not a secondary question added after the primary efficiency analysis; it is inseparable from the analysis of how prices are formed.

Digital markets require new analytical tools. Network effects, two-sided platforms, and data accumulation create winner-take-all dynamics and forms of market power that standard monopoly theory captures only partially. The regulatory response, from GAFAM antitrust investigations in Europe and the United States to Morocco’s nascent digital sovereignty policy, is still being constructed.

The next chapter examines what happens when markets fail: the institutional responses of regulation, competition policy, and the state.

What this chapter should leave with the reader

Markets create value through exchange, specialisation, and dynamic learning, but only under conditions that real markets satisfy imperfectly.

Price formation varies by market structure. In agricultural and commodity markets, prices clear the market. In industrial markets, firms set price as a markup over variable costs. The markup depends on the degree of monopoly, Kalecki’s insight that links market structure directly to income distribution.

Market structures range from perfect competition (price-taking, zero economic profit in the long run) through monopolistic competition and oligopoly to monopoly. Each generates different price levels, profit rates, and distributional outcomes.

Oligopolistic interdependence is analysed through game theory: Nash equilibrium, the prisoner’s dilemma, Cournot, Bertrand, and Stackelberg models each illuminate different dimensions of strategic interaction.

The labour market is a market of a different kind: wages are rigid downward, monopsony power suppresses wages below competitive levels, and labour market segmentation produces the paradox of high graduate unemployment alongside low-wage informal employment.

Digital markets are governed by network effects, two-sided platform logic, and data economics, creating structural monopolies that require new regulatory frameworks beyond the standard competition policy toolkit.

Further Reading

Kalecki, M. (1943). “Political Aspects of Full Employment.” Political Quarterly. The most important short paper in political economy of the 20th century. Argues that capitalism systematically resists full employment because it undermines the disciplinary power of unemployment over labour.

Robinson, J. (1933). The Economics of Imperfect Competition. Macmillan. The foundational analysis of monopolistic competition and imperfect markets. Robinson demonstrated that most real markets involve P > MC and traced the distributional consequences.

Card, D. and Krueger, A. (1994). “Minimum Wages and Employment.” American Economic Review. The empirical demonstration that minimum wage increases need not reduce employment, because monopsony is widespread in labour markets.

Tirole, J. (2014). Nobel Prize Lecture, “Market Power and Regulation.” The most accessible summary of Tirole’s work on two-sided markets, platform economics, and regulatory design.

Zuboff, S. (2019). The Age of Surveillance Capitalism. PublicAffairs. The systematic account of how data-driven business models extract value from human behaviour. Essential for understanding digital market power.

Conseil de la Concurrence du Maroc. Annual Reports. The primary empirical source on competition in Moroccan markets: cement, telecoms, hydrocarbons, food distribution.

Akerlof, G. (1970). “The Market for Lemons.” Quarterly Journal of Economics. The foundational paper on information asymmetry and adverse selection, why information failures can destroy entire markets.
