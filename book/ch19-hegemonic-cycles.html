21  Chapter 19 — Hegemonic Cycles and the Long Run: A Concluding Essay

Braudel, Arrighi, and the structural coordinates of Morocco’s century

“The world is as it is.” — V.S. Naipaul

“The world is not as it is. It is as it has been made — and it can be remade.” — after James Baldwin

The purpose of this concluding chapter

This chapter is not a summary. The preceding eighteen chapters have done the analytical work: building the tools, demonstrating the mechanisms, anchoring them in Moroccan reality. This final chapter does something different, it takes the long view. It asks the question that only a historical perspective can answer: where does Morocco’s present come from, and what structural forces will shape its future?

The chapter draws on Braudel’s temporal architecture, Arrighi’s hegemonic cycles, Wallerstein’s world-systems analysis, and Polanyi’s double movement to build a reading of Morocco’s position in the world economy that transcends the analytical categories of any single discipline. It is deliberately essay-like in register, synthesising rather than demonstrating, arguing rather than proving, and opening toward the questions that no textbook can close.

The intellectual ambition is the book’s signature: navigating the complexity of the phenomenon through the simplicity of the statement, not by reducing complexity but by finding the level of abstraction at which structural patterns become visible without distorting the specificity that gives them meaning.

Prologue: The Question that Every Chapter Approached

Throughout this book, a question has been circling just beneath the surface of every chapter’s technical analysis. In Chapter 3, when we asked why some firms grow while others stagnate, the deeper question was about the institutional conditions under which productive investment becomes rational. In Chapter 8, when we traced the anatomy of financial crises from Minsky’s endogenous instability to Fisher’s debt deflation spiral, the deeper question was about the relationship between financial accumulation and productive investment, and why capitalism repeatedly substitutes the former for the latter. In Chapter 10, when we diagnosed Morocco’s assembly paradox, the deeper question was about who captures value from industrial activity and why the distribution persistently favours those with technological leverage over those with labour and natural resources. In Chapter 12, when we traced the asymmetry between the dollar’s “exorbitant privilege” and Morocco’s “original sin” of foreign-currency debt, the deeper question was about the political architecture of the international monetary system and whose interests it serves.

That recurring deeper question is the subject of this chapter. It asks: what structural forces, operating at timescales longer than any policy cycle, determine the constraints within which Morocco’s economic choices are made? And what does a long-run reading of those forces suggest about the possibilities available to Morocco in the coming decades?

Part I — The Three Temporal Registers and Their Analytical Implications

Why economists need Braudel

Economic analysis, as practised in the mainstream tradition, operates primarily in the short run. Quarterly GDP figures, monthly inflation readings, annual budget balances: the temporal horizon of most policy analysis is one to five years. This is rational given the incentive structures facing economists and policymakers, elections happen every four years, bond markets react to next quarter’s current account, but it systematically misses the structural forces that operate on longer timescales and that ultimately determine whether short-run policies achieve their objectives.

Fernand Braudel’s contribution to economic thinking was methodological: he insisted that historical reality operates simultaneously at three temporal registers that require different analytical instruments and reveal different truths.

The longue durée (centuries-long duration) is the domain of geographic and structural constraints that change so slowly they appear almost fixed within a human lifetime. Morocco’s Atlantic and Mediterranean geography, its position at the intersection of European, African, and Arab-Islamic civilisational zones, its control of the Gibraltar approach, its exceptional solar and wind resources, is a longue durée reality. No policy created it; no single policy can eliminate it. It is the geological and climatic foundation on which Morocco’s economic possibilities are built. A development strategy that ignores the longue durée may be technically coherent while being fundamentally misconceived: investing in activities that fight the structural geography rather than leveraging it.

The conjuncture (decade-to-generation duration) is the domain of economic cycles, institutional regimes, and technological paradigms. The current conjuncture includes: the energy transition (a thirty-year restructuring of global energy systems that is reshaping comparative advantages); the demographic dividend (Morocco’s young population, an asset if education and employment are adequate, a liability if they are not); the hegemonic transition from American unipolarity toward multipolarity; and the digital production revolution that is progressively automating the assembly activities on which Morocco’s industrial base currently rests. These conjunctural forces cannot be individually controlled, but they can be anticipated, and strategic choices can be designed to ride their momentum rather than resist it.

The événement (months-to-years duration) is the domain of political decisions, natural disasters, and financial shocks. The COVID pandemic, the Ukraine war’s food price transmission, the 2023 Al Haouz earthquake: these events are consequential and demand policy responses. But they do not alter Morocco’s longue durée geographic assets or its conjunctural positioning. A government that exhausts its analytical energy on event-level responses without building the structural capabilities that conjunctural windows demand is consuming its capital without investing it.

The implication of this tripartite temporal architecture for Morocco’s strategic analysis: the most important policy choices are those that align with the conjuncture’s momentum while building on the longue durée advantages. Green hydrogen investment aligns with the energy transition conjuncture while building on Morocco’s solar and wind longue durée. Battery materials investment aligns with the EV conjuncture while building on the phosphate longue durée. Neither is possible without the institutional and industrial capability investments that the preceding chapters have analysed.

Part II — Arrighi’s Cycles: Where the American Century Stands

The four cycles revisited

Giovanni Arrighi’s The Long Twentieth Century (1994) proposed that the history of capitalism can be read as a succession of systemic cycles of accumulation, each centred on a state-capital alliance that organises world commerce and finance during its ascendancy phase before entering its financial expansion phase and eventually yielding to a new challenger.

The Genoese cycle (15th-16th centuries) demonstrated that financial capitalism, the ability to control capital flows without territorial dominance, can generate disproportionate returns for those who hold the nodes through which capital must pass. The Genoese bankers financed Spanish and Portuguese empires without governing territories of their own, capturing a rent on the capital circuits of the Iberian world-economy. Their lesson: the most powerful position in a capitalist system is not ownership of production but control of the financial infrastructure through which production is financed.

The Dutch cycle (17th century) added institutional innovation to financial sophistication: the VOC, the Amsterdam Bourse, the Bank of Amsterdam, the Treaty of Westphalia. The Dutch invented the organisational forms, joint-stock companies, secondary markets, stable reserve currency, that all subsequent cycles would use. Their characteristic contribution was the institutionalisation of capitalism: creating the legal and financial architecture that makes capital accumulation scalable and transferable across time and space.

The British cycle (18th-19th centuries) added industrial production and free trade ideology to the financial and institutional innovations of predecessors. Britain’s productive supremacy in textiles, steel, and engineering provided the material foundation for its financial dominance (the gold-backed pound sterling as world reserve currency) and its ideological dominance (free trade as the universal prescription, implemented after Britain’s industrial supremacy was secure enough to benefit from open markets).

The American cycle (20th-21st centuries) added mass production, multilateral institution-building, and the dollar standard. The American cycle’s material expansion phase (1945-1970) was the most productive in capitalist history: reconstruction of Europe and Japan created new markets; Fordist mass production generated the wages that sustained mass consumption; the Bretton Woods system provided monetary stability; and the United Nations framework provided political legitimacy. The American cycle’s financial expansion phase (1970s onward) has progressively substituted financial engineering for productive investment, dollar printing for value creation, and regulatory arbitrage for genuine innovation.

The signal crisis and its consequences

Arrighi dates the signal crisis of the American cycle to 1971: Nixon’s closure of the gold window, which acknowledged that the United States could not maintain its Bretton Woods commitments without constraining the domestic monetary expansion that its political economy required. The subsequent fifty years have been the financial expansion phase of the American cycle: maintaining dollar dominance through financial mechanisms, Treasury bond recycling, dollar weaponisation, financial market innovation, rather than through productive supremacy.

The paradox of this phase: American financial dominance has been strengthened, not weakened, by the end of dollar-gold convertibility. Because no alternative exists as a store of value and medium of exchange for international trade, the world has continued accumulating dollar assets even as their gold backing disappeared and as the United States ran persistent current account deficits that would require balance-of-payments adjustment in any other economy. The “exorbitant privilege” analysed in Chapter 12 is the institutional expression of Arrighi’s M’-phase logic: the hegemon extracts systemic rents through its control of the monetary circuit rather than through the productivity of its production.

The terminal crisis of the American cycle is unfolding at irregular pace. 2008 was a signal of terminal crisis dynamics: the leading financial complex itself generated the global crisis, inverting the hegemon’s traditional role as systemic stabiliser. The 2016-2020 American withdrawal from multilateralism, rejecting the rules-based international order that the United States had constructed, is further evidence of a hegemon less willing to provide the public goods that its hegemony required. The China-United States technology conflict is the most direct expression of the hegemonic transition: a rising challenger whose productive and technological capacities now rival the incumbent in semiconductors, AI, electric vehicles, and renewable energy is meeting an incumbent that uses regulatory, financial, and military instruments to defend its position.

The transition and its structural uncertainty

Every Arrighian hegemonic transition has involved a period of systemic chaos, Braudel’s term for the interregnum between the decline of one hegemonic order and the consolidation of the next. The period from the signal crisis to the terminal crisis can span decades, during which old institutions lose legitimacy without being replaced, geopolitical competition intensifies, and the rules of the international economic game become contested rather than settled.

Morocco has lived through one hegemonic transition already: the shift from British to American hegemony, through which the Protectorate was eventually ended as American post-war anti-colonialism replaced British imperial legitimacy. The current transition, from American unipolarity toward multipolarity, creates a different but analogous opportunity structure for a middle power positioned at the intersection of multiple great power spheres of influence.

The specific opportunity of systemic transitions for middle powers: during stable hegemonic periods, the rules are fixed and smaller actors adapt to them. During transitions, rules are contested, new institutions are being built, and actors with genuine assets and credible governance can negotiate terms of engagement rather than simply accepting dictation. Morocco’s ability to engage simultaneously with European, Chinese, Gulf, and African partners, without full alignment with any, positions it to extract value from the transition’s multi-directional character that would not be available in a stable hegemonic environment.

Morocco in the hegemonic cycles: three moments of integration

Morocco’s integration into the capitalist world-economy has occurred through three structurally distinct moments, each conditioned by the phase of the hegemonic cycle in which it occurred and each leaving structural residues that shape the possibilities available today.

The first moment (1830-1912) coincided with the late phase of the British cycle, its financialisation period. European commercial capital, seeking new accumulation frontiers as European domestic markets saturated, extended credit, extracted tariff concessions through unequal treaties, and established commercial networks across the Mediterranean periphery. Morocco’s fiscal dependence on European loans (particularly the 1904 loans that directly preceded the Protectorate) illustrates the structural dynamic: debt creates the leverage that converts economic presence into political control. The Treaty of Fez was not primarily a military conquest, it was the culmination of a fiscal dependency mechanism that the British cycle’s financial expansion phase constructed across its periphery.

The second moment (1912-1956) coincided with the American cycle’s ascent and the British cycle’s terminal crisis. The Protectorate restructured Morocco’s productive geography to serve French metropolitan accumulation: phosphate extraction, commercial agriculture on the Atlantic plains, minimal industrial development. The Maroc utile distinction was not a geographic discovery but a political allocation of infrastructure that concentrated accumulation potential in the Atlantic corridor and simultaneously converted interior Morocco into a labour reservoir. This spatial economy was rational from the perspective of French metropolitan capital in the American cycle’s material expansion phase, which was rebuilding Europe’s industrial capacity and needed raw materials and labour.

The third moment (1956-present) coincides with the American cycle’s late financial expansion phase. The Volcker shock’s transfer of adjustment costs to peripheral debtors directly produced Morocco’s 1983 Structural Adjustment Programme. The subsequent insertion into European automotive and aerospace value chains occurred during the phase when European firms were reorganising their supply chains toward lower-cost adjacent locations, a strategy that served European capital’s need to respond to Asian competition by accessing lower-wage assembly capacity without the logistical or regulatory complexity of far-shore operations.

In Arrighi’s framework, each of Morocco’s integration moments was shaped by the hegemonic cycle’s dynamics at least as much as by Morocco’s autonomous choices. But each moment also created structural capabilities that the next moment could build on: the agricultural infrastructure of the Protectorate became the basis for the Plan Maroc Vert’s modern agricultural strategy; the phosphate industry nationalised in 1975 became OCP’s platform for the fertiliser upgrading that is now extending toward battery materials; the automotive cluster inserted in European value chains is now the industrial base from which EV component development is possible.

This reading suggests a structural optimism that is not naive: Morocco’s development is constrained by the structural dynamics of the hegemonic cycle in which it operates, but not determined by them. Each constraint creates pressure that, if creatively engaged rather than passively absorbed, generates new capabilities. The SAP’s destruction of Morocco’s directed credit instruments was a genuine loss of industrial policy capacity, and it generated the political and administrative learning that made Morocco a more sophisticated interlocutor with international financial institutions in subsequent decades. The automotive cluster’s current assembly-without-deepening limitation is simultaneously a demonstration that Morocco can attract world-class manufacturing investment and a specification of what the next phase of industrial policy must address.

Part III — Polanyi’s Double Movement and the Social Contract

The disembedding and re-embedding of the market

Karl Polanyi’s The Great Transformation (1944) provided a reading of 19th-century capitalism that remains as analytically powerful as when written. Polanyi argued that the attempt to create a self-regulating market, a market for labour, land, and money governed entirely by price signals without social or institutional embedding, was a historical experiment that generated its own destruction. The social dislocations produced by the “disembedding” of economic activity from social and moral constraints provoked a counter-movement of social protection: labour legislation, social insurance, banking regulation, agricultural supports. The 20th century was the history of this double movement: the expansion of market mechanisms followed by social protection reactions.

Polanyi’s framework illuminates the Washington Consensus and its aftermath with particular clarity. The structural adjustment programmes of the 1980s-1990s were a deliberate attempt to disembed markets from national social protections: reducing subsidies, privatising public enterprises, removing capital controls, liberalising labour markets. The social dislocations they produced, rising inequality, declining formal employment, withdrawal of public services, generated the backlash that Rodrik theorises as the political limits of hyperglobalisation.

For Morocco, the Polanyi reading has a direct domestic application. The progressive dismantling of Morocco’s social compact, bread subsidies, price controls on basic necessities, public enterprise employment guarantees, under structural adjustment in the 1980s and its aftermath has produced a social disembedding whose full political consequences have not yet been absorbed. The 2011 reform pressures, the Hirak movements in peripheral regions, and the persistent political salience of social equity are expressions of the Polanyian counter-movement: society demanding re-embedding of economic activity in social and moral constraints.

Morocco’s 2021-2025 social protection reform is the most significant attempt at re-embedding in the post-adjustment era: extending health insurance, building cash transfer infrastructure, moving from universal product subsidies toward targeted household transfers. If this re-embedding succeeds, if it creates a genuine social compact that distributes the gains from Morocco’s productive transformation more broadly, it will address the social contract deficit that the adjustment era created. If it fails, if the RSU targeting generates exclusions, if fiscal constraints limit its reach, if service quality fails to equalise, the Polanyian counter-movement will find other political expressions.

The structural tension at the heart of development

The Polanyi framework reveals a structural tension at the heart of Morocco’s development challenge that no policy can fully resolve: the productive transformation that development requires, structural adjustment of productive capabilities, reallocation of labour from low-productivity to high-productivity activities, integration into global value chains, generates social disruptions that destabilise the social compact that productive transformation requires.

Workers displaced from declining sectors by trade and technological change need social protection and retraining that only a functioning state can provide, but the fiscal constraints that trade integration imposes limit the state’s capacity to provide them. Communities that lose economic anchors to deindustrialisation need coordinated public investment to rebuild their economic base, but the spatial concentration logic of market economies pulls investment toward already-prosperous centres. The intergenerational human capital investment that sustaining transformation requires produces returns only after a generation, but political economy operates on electoral cycles.

These tensions cannot be dissolved; they must be managed. Managing them requires exactly the institutions this book has analysed: credible social protection that cushions transition costs, industrial policy that coordinates productive transformation without creating permanent rents, territorial policy that builds economic capability in lagging regions rather than only compensating their population for the cost of concentration, and a fiscal system that captures enough of the gains from growth to finance the public investments that growth requires.

The transition scenarios and Morocco’s strategic choice space

Arrighi and Wallerstein agree that we inhabit a historical bifurcation: a moment when multiple trajectories of the world order are simultaneously possible and when the outcome depends on the balance of forces and the quality of collective organisation. For Morocco, the strategic question is not which great power to align with but how to position itself to capture value from the transition whatever trajectory it follows.

Three scenarios frame the analytical range.

Scenario one: Chinese hegemonic cycle. China consolidates productive supremacy in advanced manufacturing (electric vehicles, semiconductors, renewable energy equipment, AI systems), the renminbi progressively internationalises, and the Belt and Road Initiative matures into a Chinese-centred institutional framework. For Morocco, this scenario implies: growing Chinese investment in strategic Moroccan assets (energy, ports, digital infrastructure); potential dependence on Chinese technology standards that could constrain relationships with European partners; and a broader space for South-South economic partnerships that Chinese hegemony would likely facilitate. The strategic risk: reproducing the structural dependency dynamic that characterised Morocco’s integration into the British and American cycles, but now centred on Beijing rather than London or Washington.

Scenario two: multipolar fragmentation. No single power achieves hegemonic consolidation. Trade and production reorganise into regional blocs (EU-Euromed, China-Asian, Americas, African Union). Morocco’s position between the EU and African blocs gives it a distinctive insertion: simultaneously a peripheral partner of the EU’s most advanced regulatory framework and a central actor in the African continental economy. The strategic opportunity: leveraging its dual insertion to be the bridge that neither bloc can fully replicate. The risk: being pulled in incompatible directions by competing regulatory regimes, supply chain standards, and geopolitical alignments that fragmentation makes increasingly costly to straddle.

Scenario three: systemic transformation. Climate change, inequality, and the AI transition generate institutional responses that go beyond a simple change of hegemonic centre: new forms of international financial architecture (a reformed SDR system, a climate finance mechanism genuinely responsive to developing country needs), new industrial models (circular economy, distributed renewable production), and new social contracts (universal basic services, democratic data governance). For Morocco, this is the scenario where the NMD’s ambitions are most fully achievable: a world that rewards sustainable productive transformation, redistributive social policy, and genuine partnership rather than purely transactional relationships is a world where Morocco’s longue durée assets (renewable energy, human geography, cultural position) are most valuable.

Morocco cannot choose which scenario materialises. It can position itself to extract value from each. The analytical framework this book provides, the four-condition industrial policy, the product space strategy, the Thirlwall growth constraint, the Minsky financial cycle awareness, the Arrighian long view, is the equipment for making that positioning intelligent rather than reactive.

Part IV — Morocco’s Long Century

Three historical moments of integration

Morocco’s integration into the world capitalist economy has occurred in three distinct moments, each corresponding to a phase of the hegemonic cycle in which it occurred.

The first moment (19th-century commercial penetration, 1830-1912) occurred during the late British cycle, as European commercial capital, principally French and British, extended its reach into Mediterranean peripheries through unequal treaties, debt leverage, and commercial presence. Morocco signed the Treaty of Fez in 1912 as a semi-peripheral country whose fiscal dependence on European creditors had progressively limited its sovereign economic policy. This is not primarily a story of external aggression, it is a story of the structural dynamics through which European capitalist expansion systematically converted independent trading polities into dependent zones of accumulation.

The second moment (the Protectorate, 1912-1956) coincided with the late British cycle’s terminal crisis and the American cycle’s ascent. The colonial economy deliberately restructured Morocco’s productive geography (the Maroc utile distinction analysed in Chapter 17) and its commodity structure (phosphate extraction, commercial agriculture, minimal industrial development) to serve French metropolitan accumulation while providing the social stability on which colonial administration depended.

The third moment (post-independence integration, 1956-present) occurred during the American cycle’s material expansion phase and then its financial expansion phase. Morocco’s structural adjustment programmes were imposed during the Volcker shock’s global balance-of-payments crisis, the moment when the American cycle’s financial expansion phase transferred its adjustment costs to peripheral debtors. Morocco’s subsequent insertion into European automotive and aerospace value chains occurred during the phase when European firms were reorganising their supply chains toward low-cost adjacent locations in response to Asian competitive pressure.

Each integration moment has been shaped by the hegemonic cycle’s dynamics more than by Morocco’s autonomous strategic choices. But each has also left structural residues, capabilities, institutions, social formations, that have shaped the possibilities available in subsequent moments. The phosphate industry nationalised in 1975 is the accumulated legacy of colonial extraction reclaimed and transformed. The automotive cluster is the accumulated legacy of structural adjustment’s export orientation redirected toward higher-complexity activities. Each strategic choice builds on the inheritance of choices made by predecessors operating in different hegemonic contexts.

What the Moroccan century requires

The analytical journey of this book converges on a set of structural requirements for Morocco’s long-run development that transcend the policy cycles of any single government or the strategic horizon of any single business plan.

Productive deepening as the condition for everything else. The book’s central analytical finding is that Morocco’s challenge is not primarily macroeconomic (inflation, fiscal balance, exchange rate) or sectoral (which industries to promote) but structural: the gap between the complexity of activities Morocco’s economy performs and the complexity of activities that would sustain Morocco’s development over the coming generation. Closing this gap requires a sustained industrial policy logic that is now well-understood analytically and has been demonstrated as feasible in Morocco’s automotive and aerospace experience. What it requires beyond the analytical is political: the coalition to implement performance conditionality against the resistance of beneficiaries of the status quo.

Social contract reconstruction as the condition for political sustainability. Morocco’s development model cannot sustain high growth rates without a more inclusive distribution of their gains. The NMD 2021 acknowledged this. The social protection reform operationalises it. But the reform’s ambition far exceeds its current fiscal capacity, and the political sustainability of the required tax reforms, broadening the base toward capital and informal income, requires a narrative of shared benefit that only visible redistribution can create. The Polanyian double movement will not wait indefinitely for the re-embedding to materialise.

Territorial cohesion as the condition for national coherence. Myrdal’s cumulative causation logic, left unaddressed, produces the territorial polarisation that Rodríguez-Pose shows generates political fragmentation. Morocco’s interior and southern regions are not simply less productive versions of the coast, they are territories with distinct ecological, cultural, and economic assets (traditional crafts, agropastoral systems, underground water economies, renewable energy potential) that deserve productive development strategies adapted to their specificities, not merely social transfer programmes that compensate for the cost of their economic marginalisation.

Institutional coherence as the binding constraint. The analytical frameworks this book has developed, four conditions, product space, HRV diagnostics, Thirlwall constraint, Minsky cycle, all converge on the same institutional diagnosis: Morocco’s binding constraint is not the absence of strategy but the absence of the institutional infrastructure that converts strategy into implementation. The NMD has a coherent strategic vision. The PAI has identified the right productive sectors. OCP’s diversification is proceeding. But the conditionality mechanisms that would make industrial policy discipline real, the evaluation systems that would distinguish effective programmes from rent-distributing ones, the territorial governance architecture that would enable regionally differentiated strategies, and the fiscal reform that would provide the revenues to finance the social contract are all works in progress. These are not peripheral details; they are the operational architecture that determines whether Morocco’s analytical clarity about its development model becomes the lived reality of its development trajectory.

Strategic sovereignty as the goal of international integration. The hegemonic transition creates a moment of exceptional strategic opportunity for middle powers willing to position themselves actively. Morocco’s assets, renewable energy, phosphate chemistry, European proximity, African hub, are real and defensible. Whether they translate into genuine productive capability and geopolitical influence or remain comparative advantages that others capture depends entirely on the quality of the industrial policy, the institutional capacity, and the political coalition that Morocco assembles in the coming decade.

Epilogue: The Signature

This book opened with an ambition that deserves reiteration as a conclusion: navigating the complexity of the phenomenon through the simplicity of the statement.

The complexity is genuine. Morocco’s economy operates simultaneously at the level of a small textile firm in Fès navigating credit constraints and supply chain disruption, at the level of OCP’s global fertiliser market positioning, at the level of BAM’s exchange rate liberalisation calibration, and at the level of the structural dynamics through which the Atlantic coast concentrates opportunity while the Rif and the Atlas watch their young people leave. These levels are analytically distinct, they require different tools, different data, different conceptual frameworks. But they are causally connected: the small firm’s credit constraint is partly a consequence of the banking system’s orientation toward large connected clients; OCP’s positioning is partly a consequence of the colonial infrastructure that concentrated phosphate development on the Atlantic coast; BAM’s liberalisation is partly constrained by the dollar system’s asymmetries; and the Rif’s outmigration is partly the accumulated result of seven decades of investment patterns that reproduced the Protectorate’s spatial economy.

The simplicity is a methodological commitment, not a denial of complexity. It is the conviction that the most powerful analytical insights are those that reveal structural patterns with sufficient clarity that they can guide action, not by providing algorithms but by sharpening perception. When a student understands Myrdal’s backwash mechanism, they see differently the rural-urban migration statistics they read in HCP reports. When a policymaker understands Fisher’s debt deflation spiral, they respond differently to the first signs of asset price contraction. When a business strategist understands Hirschman’s inducement linkages, they design differently the supplier development programme their firm needs.

The multiplication of perspectives, mainstream and heterodox, Moroccan and comparative, micro and macro, short-run and long-run, is not eclecticism. It is the recognition that economic reality is too multidimensional to be captured from any single angle. Ricardo and Prebisch are both right about different dimensions of international trade. Fama and Minsky are both right about different market regimes. Solow and Lewis are both right about different phases of the same development process. The analyst who can hold these multiple perspectives simultaneously, not as contradictions to be resolved but as complementary lenses to be brought to bear on specific questions, is better equipped than one who has mastered a single framework to perfection.

Morocco stands at a structural inflection point. The hegemonic transition is real. The energy transition is real. The African integration opportunity is real. The social contract deficit is real. The territorial polarisation is real. None of these structural realities are deterministic, they create possibilities, not outcomes. Whether Morocco captures the possibilities that its longue durée advantages, its conjunctural moment, and its accumulated institutional capacity create depends on choices: choices about industrial policy conditionality, fiscal reform, social protection quality, territorial investment, and the terms on which Morocco integrates into a world economy undergoing its deepest structural reorganisation in a generation.

This book has tried to provide the analytical equipment for those choices. The choices themselves belong to Morocco.

The book’s permanent acquisitions

Finance and macroeconomics: A financial asset is a promise on uncertain future flows. Minsky’s stability-generates-instability cycle is the anatomy of every financial crisis. The international monetary system is an asymmetric hierarchy, not a club of equals. The Thirlwall constraint connects external balance to growth potential.

Production and industrial policy: All successful industrialisers used active industrial policy before imposing free trade on latecomers. The four conditions (real coordination failure, institutional capacity, conditional reciprocity, credible exit) separate learning from rents. The product space maps what is possible from where you are.

Distribution and social contract: Inequality is structurally reproduced through market rents, labour market segmentation, wealth-friendly taxation, and unequal access to quality public services. Pre-distribution is more efficient than redistribution ex post. The Polanyian double movement warns that disembedded markets generate their own social counter-movement.

Geography and strategy: Agglomeration creates productivity and exclusion simultaneously. Cumulative causation means markets tend to deepen rather than correct territorial inequality. Morocco’s macrocephaly is colonial in origin and cumulative in its reproduction, both politically correctable.

The long run: Braudel’s three temporal registers require different analytical instruments. Arrighi’s hegemonic cycles show that the dollar standard is a late phase of a systemic cycle, not a permanent equilibrium. Morocco’s strategic window coincides with the transition’s opening, and windows close.

The signature: navigating the complexity of the phenomenon through the simplicity of the statement. Not by reducing complexity but by finding the level of abstraction at which structural patterns become visible without distorting the specificity that gives them meaning.
