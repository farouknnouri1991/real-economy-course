10  Chapter 8 — Financial Markets and Fragility

Promises, valuation, architecture, and endogenous instability

“When the capital development of a country becomes a by-product of the activities of a casino, the job is likely to be ill-done.” — John Maynard Keynes, The General Theory (1936)

“Stability is destabilising.” — Hyman Minsky

The guiding thread of this chapter
  1. A financial asset is a promise, not a good. It is a contractual commitment on uncertain future flows. Financial markets do not exchange present wealth, they organise the circulation of promises on what does not yet exist. This makes them sensitive to collective narratives and structurally prone to disconnecting prices from fundamentals.
  2. Efficient market theory is a hypothesis, not a fact. Fama’s three forms, weak, semi-strong, strong, describe an ideal benchmark, not the actual behaviour of markets. Behavioural finance documents systematic and predictable deviations. Robert Shiller’s narrative economics shows that collective stories, not rational calculations, drive asset prices.
  3. The Minsky cycle is the anatomy of every financial crisis. Stability breeds risk-taking, risk-taking breeds fragility, fragility breeds crisis. This cycle is not an accident, it is the endogenous product of capitalist financial dynamics. Understanding it is the precondition for reading any financial crisis.
  4. Crises are contagion events, not just fundamental deteriorations. A financially sound country can be destroyed by a crisis it did not cause, because it shares an asset category with fragile countries. Fisher’s debt deflation spiral shows how individually rational decisions aggregate into collective catastrophe.
  5. The architecture of financial systems determines how crises propagate. Shadow banking, derivatives, agency conflicts in rating agencies, and “too big to fail” banks are not peripheral features, they are the transmission mechanisms that transform local credit problems into systemic collapses.
  6. Morocco has not experienced a systemic crisis, yet. Its relatively closed capital account, solid prudential ratios, and diversified financing sources have provided insulation. But the mechanisms are identical, only the scale differs. The Minsky cycle operated in Morocco’s real estate market of 2007-2013 with textbook precision.

The Map

The previous chapter showed how money is created by banks, how central banks govern the monetary system, and why financial markets are structurally unstable. That chapter introduced Minsky’s central insight; this one builds its full architecture.

Finance is simultaneously the indispensable nervous system of a modern economy and its most persistent source of systemic instability. Without finance, no long-horizon productive investment, no roads, no factories, no agricultural cooperatives, would be possible, because those who have savings and those who have investment projects are almost never the same people. With finance, the mechanisms that enable this intermediation also create the conditions for periodic collapses that destroy real economic activity, devastate households, and erase decades of productive accumulation.

The chapter advances in seven movements. First, the philosophical foundations: what finance is and what it does to time. Second, the instruments and markets: how assets are valued and what determines their prices. Third, the efficiency hypothesis and its critics: from Fama to Shiller via Kahneman. Fourth, the institutional architecture and its regulation: who sits between the saver and the borrower, and with what consequences. Fifth, Minsky’s theory of endogenous instability, the most important single framework for understanding financial crises. Sixth, eight major crises analysed through this framework. Seventh, Morocco’s financial system: its structure, strengths, fragilities, and what the crisis literature implies for its future.

11 Part I — What Finance Really Is

11.1 A financial asset is a promise

Before any instrument, before any crisis, before any regulation, one must understand what a financial asset is. It is not a tangible good. It is a promise: a contractual commitment on future uncertain flows. Buying a Moroccan Treasury bond means believing the state will repay in 2035. Acquiring an Attijariwafa Bank share means betting the bank will continue distributing dividends. Signing a life insurance policy means trusting the company will still be solvent in twenty years. Entering a phosphate futures contract means mutually committing to a price nobody controls.

The debt recognition between cousins

Your cousin borrows 5,000 dirhams and writes on a piece of paper: “I will pay you back 5,500 dirhams in one year.” You have just created a financial asset. This paper now has a value, you could even sell it to a third party willing to wait for repayment. A government bond, a company share, a bank loan are nothing more than this: a promise of future flows, whose value depends on the confidence one places in the one who promises. The entire financial edifice, the Casablanca Stock Exchange, Treasury bills, sukuk, rests on this simple idea: exchanging certain money today for a promise of money tomorrow. Everything else is refinement of this elementary gesture.

This definition has a decisive consequence: financial markets do not exchange present goods. They organise the circulation of promises on future flows. What is bought and sold on a stock exchange is not a kilogram of wheat but a right over something that has not yet occurred. Michel Aglietta captured this precisely: “Finance does not reflect the world; it organises its expectation.” On a goods market, a valuation error costs a missed transaction. On a financial market, it builds a bubble, because collective belief creates prices, and prices in turn influence behaviours. Real estate “that can only rise,” technology “that will revolutionise everything”: these narratives are not mere analytical errors. They are self-fulfilling prophecies that feed on themselves until reality breaks them.

11.2 Confidence as public institutional infrastructure

Finance presents itself as a space of pure market freedom. It routinely forgets to mention that it rests entirely on public institutions without which it would collapse within hours. Property rights, securities registries, commercial courts, regulators, lenders of last resort: without this infrastructure, no financial contract can be concluded or executed. The freedom of financial markets is built on a foundation of public institutional investment that the markets themselves did not construct and cannot maintain.

This becomes visible precisely when the infrastructure wavers. In September 2008, Lehman Brothers’ bankruptcy did not merely create losses for its creditors. It paralysed global interbank markets because it broke mutual trust: every bank wondered whether its counterparties could fall next, and refused to lend until the answer was known. It was not the real economy that first collapsed; it was institutional confidence, and the real economy followed. The speed of contagion, from Lehman’s bankruptcy filing on September 15 to a global credit freeze within two weeks, illustrates how dependent the entire system is on the shared confidence that underlies every financial transaction.

Katharina Pistor’s The Code of Capital (2019) sharpens this point: it is not merely public institutions in general that underwrite finance but specifically legal institutions, the coding of assets in law that transforms mere resources into capital. Land becomes collateral only when secured property law makes it so. Corporate shares generate returns only when corporate law protects them. Derivatives contracts are enforceable only within a legal system that recognises them. Capital, in Pistor’s formulation, is a legal construction, and the legal systems that construct it are dominated by the interests of those who hold the most capital.

11.3 The four functions of finance — and their limits

Economic theory attributes four functions to finance: channelling savings toward productive projects, allocating and managing risks among agents with different preferences, pricing assets to guide capital allocation, and transforming maturities, allowing an investor to buy a twenty-year project while retaining the possibility of selling the claim tomorrow.

The daret: a bank without a bank

Ten female colleagues each put 500 dirhams per month into a common pot. Each month, one of them takes the 5,000 dirhams, in rotation, until all have had their turn. This is the daret (rotating savings club), and it is pure finance without any bank or paperwork. The one who goes first has received a loan (she collects before having contributed everything); the one who goes last has saved (she has lent to others for months). The cement is not a contract or a guarantee; it is trust and the social shame of not paying. The daret does exactly what a bank does: transform small regular contributions into a large available sum. It proves that where the formal banking system does not reach, people reinvent finance with what they have at hand, and that the formal system’s absence is not the absence of financial need but the absence of financial access.

These four functions are real. But their realisation is always partial and sometimes perverted. Savings channelling can finance speculation rather than productive investment. Risk management can concentrate risks with actors least capable of bearing them. Asset pricing produces bubbles when prices diverge durably from fundamentals. Maturity transformation creates an illusion of liquidity that can evaporate simultaneously for all during a panic. The central question is therefore not “is finance useful?”, it is. It is: under what institutional conditions does it serve the real economy, and when does it turn against it?

11.4 What finance does to time

Finance creates an economy of time: a system that makes possible transactions that the present alone would not permit. Three fundamental operations follow.

Transferring resources from present to future is saving: a household that places its cash in OPCVM or Treasury bills renounces consuming today to have resources tomorrow. Transferring resources from future to present is credit: an enterprise that borrows to buy machinery consumes today resources it will only possess after producing and selling. Without credit, only already-rich agents could invest, a structurally conservative outcome that would eliminate most transformative investment.

Making incompatible temporalities coincide is maturity transformation, the most remarkable and most dangerous function of finance. A household deposits savings at-sight (withdrawable at any moment). The bank lends these funds for twenty years to a property buyer. The bank has transformed short-term resources into long-term employment. This transformation is at the heart of the banking model, and at the heart of its fragility: if all depositors demand their money simultaneously, the bank cannot honour this without selling illiquid assets at a loss.

This temporal architecture has two profound consequences. First, someone must always bear the risk that the future disappoints expectations: the lender risks non-repayment, the shareholder risks value loss, the insurer risks higher claims than anticipated. Organising this risk-bearing, who carries it, at what price, with what protection, is the real function of financial architecture. Second, finance generates a structural tension between the short and long term: markets price assets daily, every quarterly result below expectations collapses a share price. But the most socially useful investments have horizons of thirty to fifty years. This discordance between financial markets that look six months ahead and real economies that need capital for thirty years is one of the fundamental pathologies of contemporary finance, explaining why states and public development banks remain indispensable for financing what private markets chronically underinvest in.

12 Part II — Instruments, Markets, and Valuation

12.1 The hierarchy of rights: debt, equity, derivatives

All financial assets divide into three families according to their position in the hierarchy of rights.

Debt instruments, bonds, Treasury bills, bank loans, commercial paper, are claims. The issuer promises to repay principal and interest on a predetermined schedule. The creditor is prioritised in bankruptcy, paid before shareholders. In exchange, their return is capped: whatever happens to the firm, they receive only what is contractually owed. A Moroccan Treasury bond, an OCP Eurobond, a BCP mortgage: debt instruments with radically different risk profiles but the same fundamental structure.

Capital instruments, shares, equity stakes, are ownership rights. The shareholder is co-owner, votes in general assemblies, receives dividends, and benefits from value appreciation. But their right is residual: in bankruptcy, repaid only after all creditors have been satisfied. In exchange for this superior risk, the potential gain is theoretically unlimited. This asymmetric profile, loss limited to invested capital, theoretically unlimited gain, is what fundamentally distinguishes equity investment from bond holding.

Derivatives, options, futures, swaps, CDS, are contracts whose value derives from an underlying asset without requiring its physical possession. One can buy phosphate price futures without possessing a gram of phosphate. This dissociation between contract value and physical possession is simultaneously the power of derivatives (fine risk management with little capital) and their danger (massive exposure with little own funds). The global derivatives market represented over 700 trillion dollars of notional value in 2023, seven times world GDP. This figure overstates actual risk (net risk after netting is far smaller), but illustrates the scale of disconnection between financial activity and the real economy it ostensibly serves.

12.2 The present value principle: the foundation of everything

All financial valuation rests on a single foundational principle: the value of an asset today equals the sum of all future cash flows it is expected to generate, each discounted at a rate that reflects the risk of not receiving them.

\[V_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}\]

The discount rate \(r\) incorporates two components: the risk-free rate (proxied by short-term government bill rates) and a risk premium compensating for the probability that expected flows are not received. Higher discount rates mean future cash flows are worth less today, which is why rising interest rates depress asset prices. This inverse relationship between rates and prices runs through all of finance.

The savings account versus the cousin’s taxi

You have 50,000 dirhams. Option A: a savings account at the bank, 2% guaranteed, zero risk. Option B: financing the cousin who wants to buy a taxi, who promises 15%, but might go bankrupt. The gap between 2% and 15% is not a gift: it is the price of risk. Nobody would accept the taxi’s risk for the same return as the guaranteed account. This is the entire logic of the risk-return relationship: the more a placement promises, the more it can disappoint. When a financial product promises a high return “without risk,” it is not an opportunity, it is a warning signal. The discount rate is nothing more than this: the return required to compensate for the risk that the promised flow does not arrive.

Bond valuation applies this directly. A bond paying an annual coupon \(C\) with face value \(F\) at maturity \(T\) is worth the present value of all coupon payments plus the face value:

\[P = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{C}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+r)^T}\]

If market rates rise above the coupon rate, the bond price falls below par; if rates fall below the coupon rate, the price rises above par. This inverse relationship is arithmetic identity. The concept of duration measures how sensitive a bond’s price is to rate changes: a bond with duration 7 years falls approximately 7% in price when rates rise 1 percentage point. Longer bonds have higher duration and are more sensitive to rate movements, the fundamental reason why long-term bond portfolios are exposed to interest rate risk.

Equity valuation extends the same logic. The Gordon-Shapiro model values a share as the present discounted value of all future dividends growing at rate \(g\):

\[V = \frac{D_1}{r - g}\]

This formula reveals why financial markets are extremely sensitive to growth expectations: a small change in \(g\) produces a large change in valuation. It also reveals a structural tension: a firm that retains earnings for productive investment does not distribute dividends today but should generate higher future dividends. Financial markets that evaluate quarterly may price the near-term dividend cut negatively before recognising the long-term growth payoff, Keynes’s casino problem in formal dress.

12.3 Markowitz, diversification, and the CAPM

Harry Markowitz (Nobel 1990) formalised the principle of diversification: a portfolio’s risk is not the sum of its components’ risks but depends on their correlations. Combining assets that move inversely reduces total risk without sacrificing average return. There exists an efficient frontier, the set of portfolios that maximise return for a given risk level.

The umbrella-sunglasses merchant

A merchant sells umbrellas and sunglasses from the same stall. If it rains, umbrellas sell; if it shines, sunglasses sell. Whatever the weather, something sells. Without planning it, he has diversified against weather risk by combining negatively correlated goods. Markowitz received a Nobel Prize for formalising this grandmother’s wisdom in mathematics: what matters is not just the risk of each individual asset but whether their movements tend to cancel each other out. The managers of Morocco’s public pension funds, CIMR, RCAR, CMR, use variants of this framework to allocate between stocks, bonds, and real estate.

The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM, Sharpe 1964) extends this to market equilibrium: if all investors are rational and share the same information, they all hold the same optimal risky portfolio, the market portfolio. The expected return of any asset is a linear function of its beta (\(\beta\)), measuring sensitivity to market movements:

\[E(r_i) = r_f + \beta_i \cdot [E(r_m) - r_f]\]

An asset with \(\beta = 1.5\) amplifies market movements 1.5 times; its risk premium must be 1.5 times the market risk premium. This formula is the foundation of corporate cost of capital estimation worldwide. Its critical limitation: in a financial crisis, correlations between assets converge toward 1, everything falls simultaneously, exactly when diversification would be most needed. In September-October 2008, equities, corporate bonds, real estate, and hedge funds all fell together, making Markowitz’s diversification temporarily worthless.

12.4 The capital structure of firms: Modigliani-Miller and its corrections

Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller’s theorem (1958) establishes a foundational benchmark: in a world of perfect markets with no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, and no information asymmetries, a firm’s value is independent of how it finances itself. Debt and equity are perfect substitutes; the capital structure is irrelevant.

This result is deliberately unrealistic. Its pedagogical force is to make visible precisely what it sets aside: by relaxing its assumptions, one identifies why capital structure matters. Three real-world mechanisms drive the choice.

The tax shield of debt: interest payments to creditors are deductible from taxable profit; dividends to shareholders are not. This asymmetry creates a shield worth \(T_c \times D\) (tax rate times debt level). For a Moroccan firm subject to IS at 31% with 100 million MAD of debt, the annual tax shield represents approximately 31 million MAD times the interest rate, pushing firms to borrow more than the pure MM model suggests.

The costs of financial distress: beyond a certain debt level, the probability of default becomes non-negligible. Direct costs (legal proceedings, forced-sale depreciation) and indirect costs (clients avoiding long-term contracts with fragile firms, suppliers demanding upfront payment, talented managers leaving) increase with indebtedness and depress value.

Capital structure signalling: in a world of imperfect information, issuing debt signals confidence, managers who commit to fixed interest payments reveal their belief in future cash flows. Issuing new equity can signal that management believes the stock is overvalued. Addoha and Alliances, Moroccan real estate developers that expanded massively on debt during the 2007-2013 boom and faced severe financial distress when markets turned, illustrate how the costs of financial distress operate in real cases.

13 Part III — Market Efficiency, Behavioural Finance, and the Power of Narratives

13.1 Fama’s efficient market hypothesis: three forms

Eugene Fama’s efficient market hypothesis (EMH) holds that asset prices fully reflect all available information. If this is true, no investor can systematically earn returns above the market, any attempt to exploit available information is already priced in. The hypothesis comes in three forms of increasing strength.

The weak form holds that prices fully reflect all historical price information. Technical analysis, seeking to predict future prices from past price patterns, cannot generate systematic excess returns. The evidence for the weak form is fairly strong in liquid markets: price patterns documented in one period tend to disappear once published, as arbitrageurs exploit them.

The semi-strong form holds that prices reflect all publicly available information: financial statements, macroeconomic data, management announcements, analyst reports. If true, fundamental analysis, studying public information to find undervalued firms, cannot generate systematic excess returns. The evidence is more mixed: some information categories (small firm size, high book-to-market ratios) seem to generate persistent excess returns even after adjusting for known risk factors, which Fama and French (1993) incorporated into their multi-factor model rather than interpreting as inefficiency.

The strong form holds that prices reflect all information including private insider information. This is the strongest and most clearly violated form: documented cases of insider trading generating excess returns are numerous enough to motivate the entire regulatory apparatus against it.

For Morocco’s Bourse des Valeurs de Casablanca, the relevant efficiency level is probably below even the semi-strong form. With approximately 76 listed companies, low trading volumes, concentrated institutional ownership, limited analyst coverage, and documented insider trading incidents, the BVC exhibits characteristics associated with weak-form inefficiency: information diffuses slowly, liquidity is thin, and prices in some thinly traded stocks can move significantly on small orders. This structural inefficiency is not merely a market failure in the technical sense, it is a barrier to productive capital allocation that reduces the BVC’s contribution to Morocco’s development financing.

13.2 Behavioural finance: predictable irrationality

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (Nobel 2002) documented systematic deviations from rational behaviour that are predictable rather than random. If deviations were random, they would cancel out in aggregate and markets might still be efficient. But if agents systematically deviate in the same direction, markets can misprice systematically.

Loss aversion causes agents to weight losses approximately twice as much as equivalent gains. An investor who has lost 10,000 dirhams experiences roughly twice the pain of the pleasure from an equivalent gain, which leads to holding losing positions too long (to avoid realising the loss) and selling winning positions too early (to lock in the gain). These patterns are the opposite of rational profit maximisation.

Overconfidence leads agents to underestimate their own uncertainty. Fund managers consistently estimate their stock-picking ability above the median, a statistical impossibility. Corporate executives overestimate their project’s probability of success. Entrepreneurs launching businesses overestimate their survival probability. This systematic overconfidence generates excessive risk-taking.

Herding and social proof cause agents to imitate others’ actions rather than processing information independently. When prices are rising, the apparent fact that others are buying reads as confirmation of value, which drives prices further, attracting more buyers, until the narrative exhausts its supply of new believers.

The real estate fever where everyone buys because everyone buys

“Buy land now, it only goes up!” Everyone repeats it, so everyone buys, and prices actually rise, which “proves” everyone was right, attracting even more buyers. Until the day when no one is left to buy at the inflated prices reached, and everything collapses: those who bought at the top are ruined. This is a bubble, and its engine is not the cold reason of textbooks but herd psychology, fear of missing out, imitation, the illusion that “this time is different.” The behavioural finance research program studies precisely these moments when markets stop being rational calculators and become emotional crowds. From tulip bulbs to Bitcoin, the same fever replays: what rises because it is rising eventually falls because it is falling.

13.3 Shiller and narrative economics

Robert Shiller (Nobel 2013) showed empirically that stock markets are excessively volatile relative to what changes in fundamental values would justify, and that price-earnings ratios are mean-reverting over long periods. His CAPE (Cyclically Adjusted Price-to-Earnings ratio, averaging earnings over 10 years to filter cyclical fluctuations) predicted both the 2001 and 2008 crises with remarkable accuracy, not their precise timing, but the unsustainability of the valuation levels.

Shiller’s later work on narrative economics argues that the viral spread of compelling economic stories is the central driver of economic fluctuations. Stories about new technologies, new growth models, new asset classes spread through populations the way epidemics do, following logistic curves of contagion, peaking, and declining as competing narratives emerge. The “rational expectations” framework assumes agents process information correctly and update their beliefs optimally. Shiller shows that what agents actually do is adopt and spread stories, and that these stories, not information updates, move prices.

The practical implication is disturbing: financial markets are not just information-processing machines but narrative-propagation systems. This makes them simultaneously essential for coordinating economic expectations and profoundly prone to collective errors, especially when an exciting narrative (internet companies, Chinese growth, emerging market demographics, Moroccan real estate) provides a compelling story for asset appreciation that feeds on itself until reality imposes a correction.

14 Part IV — Architecture and Regulation

14.1 Intermediated versus direct finance

There are two fundamentally different ways to organise the financing of an economy, reflecting two philosophies of risk management.

In intermediated finance, risk is absorbed and transformed by an intermediary. The bank collects short-term household deposits and extends long-term loans. It assumes the credit risk exposure on its own balance sheet. The logic is mutualisation: a bank managing 100,000 mortgage loans can statistically estimate the annual default rate and maintain adequate provisions. This mutualisation works for idiosyncratic risks (specific to each borrower) but fails for systemic risks (hitting all borrowers simultaneously). During a severe recession, defaults occur simultaneously, diversification no longer protects, and the entire capital buffer is consumed at once.

In direct finance, risk is assumed directly by the investor without ex-ante mutualisation. Regulation compensates for the absent protective intermediary through information transparency: regular financial publications, independent audits, insider trading prohibition. Anglo-Saxon economies have historically privileged direct finance; continental European economies and Morocco have privileged intermediated finance.

14.2 Shadow banking: banks without safety nets

Shadow banking encompasses entities performing banking functions without being subject to banking regulation: money market funds, securitisation vehicles, hedge funds, consumer finance companies, fintech lenders. These entities benefit neither from public deposit insurance nor from access to the lender of last resort. They take on the structural fragility of banking, short-term liabilities funding long-term assets, without the prudential safeguards that regulated banks must maintain.

Gary Gorton’s analysis of the 2007-2008 crisis (Slapped by the Invisible Hand, 2010) shows that it was essentially a bank run on the shadow banking system: a wholesale funding panic in which institutional investors simultaneously refused to roll over short-term collateralised loans to shadow banking entities whose underlying asset quality had become uncertain. The mechanism was identical to a 1930s depositor run, except that it operated in opacity, through instruments that most regulators and bank executives did not understand until the crisis made their interconnections visible.

14.3 Rating agencies and the conflict of interest at the core

Credit rating agencies (Moody’s, S&P, Fitch) synthesise the credit risk of an issuer into a rating that conditions market access and financing costs. Their structural conflict of interest, they are paid by the issuers they rate, produced the most spectacular failures of the 2008 crisis: mortgage-backed securities built from subprime loans of poor quality received AAA ratings, enabling institutional investors whose mandates restricted them to high-rated instruments to hold these assets at massive scale.

The regulatory response after 2008, requiring registration of agencies, enhanced disclosure obligations, and liability for negligent rating, has been partially effective. But the fundamental conflict of interest remains: issuers continue to pay for ratings, and the oligopolistic structure of the industry (three agencies dominate globally) limits competitive pressure.

For Morocco, sovereign ratings directly determine external borrowing costs. Morocco’s rating (approximately Ba1/BB+ from Moody’s and Fitch in 2023) constrains the terms at which the Treasury can issue Eurobonds: each downgrade by one notch translates into tens of additional basis points of cost on international borrowings, with immediate fiscal consequences.

14.4 Regulation: Basel III and its architecture

The Basel Accords represent the international architecture of bank regulation: Basel I (1988), Basel II (2004), Basel III (2010-2019). The Capital Adequacy Ratio requires that own funds represent at least 8% of risk-weighted assets (higher under national and Basel III floors). The Liquidity Coverage Ratio measures the capacity to withstand 30 days of net outflows under stress through liquid assets. The Net Stable Funding Ratio requires long-term assets to be funded by stable long-term resources.

Together, these ratios aim to make banks capable of absorbing losses without recourse to public funds. The key insight driving Basel III: banks that are well-capitalised can absorb losses from their own resources rather than requiring taxpayer bailouts. The political economy of bank regulation shows, however, that banks consistently resist capital increases, arguing, not without some truth, that higher capital requirements reduce their lending capacity and raise the cost of credit to the real economy.

Bank Al-Maghrib has progressively implemented Basel III standards since 2013. Moroccan banks display comfortable aggregate ratios: average CAR above 13% (against the 12% regulatory minimum), LCR above 100%. But aggregate ratios mask disparities and do not capture all risk. The NPL (non-performing loan) ratio represented approximately 8-9% of total loans in 2022-2023, elevated, reflecting COVID aftermath and sectoral difficulties in real estate, construction, and SMEs.

14.5 Too big to fail and the institutionalisation of moral hazard

The “too big to fail” problem is one of the most corrosive institutional dynamics in modern finance. A bank whose failure would impose catastrophic systemic costs on the entire economy is implicitly guaranteed by the state, its creditors know they will be bailed out rather than absorbing losses. This guarantee, implicit but credible, distorts incentives: it allows large banks to fund themselves at lower rates (not reflecting their actual risk, since the state absorbs the tail risk), encouraging excessive leverage and risk-taking.

The 2008 crisis made this implicit guarantee explicit and quantified: the US government committed approximately 700 billion dollars through TARP, the Federal Reserve provided trillions in emergency lending, and AIG alone received 182 billion dollars in direct federal support. The message to the banking system was clear: large enough, interconnected enough, and the state will rescue you regardless of the risks you took. Post-crisis reforms, higher capital requirements for systemically important banks, resolution planning requirements, restrictions on proprietary trading, have partially addressed the problem. They have not eliminated it.

14.6 Alternative regulatory proposals

Three regulatory reform proposals have been extensively discussed since 2008, each addressing a different dimension of financial system pathology.

The financial transaction tax (FTT, or “Tobin tax”) would levy each financial transaction at a small rate (0.01-0.1%). It would discourage high-frequency trading (which generates liquidity in normal times but can amplify crashes) without penalising long-term investors, and would generate substantial revenues. France and Italy have adopted FTTs since 2012. The deterrent effect is real but incomplete: a tax coordinated at EU or G20 level would be far more effective than a national tax alone, since financial activity can relocate to avoid jurisdiction-specific levies.

Banking separation, separating deposit-taking banks from investment banks, on the model of the Glass-Steagall Act (enacted 1933, repealed 1999), aims to prevent risks taken with guaranteed deposit resources from endangering the whole system. The post-2008 Vickers report in the UK proposed “ring-fencing” commercial banking activities rather than full separation; the Volcker Rule in the US restricted proprietary trading. Large universal banks’ lobbying substantially limited the ambition of both reforms. The fundamental argument for separation remains valid: when the guarantee backing deposits also implicitly backstops trading operations, the asymmetry between private gains and socialised losses is maximised.

Public development banks, Germany’s KfW, Brazil’s BNDES, France’s Caisse des Dépôts, Morocco’s CDG and the Fonds Mohammed VI pour l’Investissement, finance projects with high social returns but insufficient private market attractiveness. The standard justification: positive externalities (infrastructure benefits non-payers), long time horizons (returns materialise beyond private investors’ patience), and capital market failures (imperfect information prevents adequate financing of small innovative firms). Morocco’s CDG embodies this logic at national scale: not as an alternative to private finance but as a complement for the sectors where market failures create financing gaps that, if unfilled, depress long-run growth.

15 Part V — Minsky and the Endogenous Instability of Finance

15.1 The foundational hypothesis

Hyman Minsky (1919-1996) was the most important financial economist of the 20th century, and for most of that century, one of the most ignored. His central hypothesis, formulated in Stabilizing an Unstable Economy (1986), can be stated in four propositions.

First: capitalism is inherently financially unstable. Not as an accidental pathology but as a structural property: the same mechanisms that drive growth and investment during expansions necessarily create the fragility that produces crises.

Second: the financial structure of firms evolves during expansions in ways that increase systemic fragility. Minsky distinguishes three financial postures. Hedge finance: the borrower can service both interest and principal from current revenues, no dependence on asset appreciation. Speculative finance: the borrower can cover interest but must refinance principal at each maturity, exposed to liquidity risk if credit conditions tighten. Ponzi finance: the borrower can cover neither interest nor principal from current revenues; survival depends on continuous asset price appreciation. If prices fall, insolvency follows immediately.

Third: the evolution from hedge to speculative to Ponzi finance occurs endogenously during periods of stability. During calm growth, profitable borrowers encourage banks to relax credit standards. Banks competing for market share cannot maintain conservative standards without losing clients to competitors. Each bank is rationally constrained to follow its competitors even as the aggregate result is increasing systemic fragility. Individual rationality produces collective irrationality.

Fourth: stability is destabilising. The very success of capitalism in generating stable growth creates the conditions for the next crisis. Long periods without major crises encourage risk-taking, leverage expansion, and the progressive migration of borrowers toward speculative and Ponzi positions. “This time is different” becomes the prevailing cognitive frame, exactly when it is most dangerous.

15.2 The five-phase cycle

Minsky’s cycle, extended by Kindleberger (Manias, Panics and Crashes, 1978), operates in five recognisable phases.

Displacement creates a new profit opportunity: a technological innovation (railways, internet, AI), a policy shift (financial deregulation, capital account opening), a new market (emerging economies, subprime mortgages). Early investors succeed, attracting imitators.

Euphoria sets in as prices rise, debt accumulates, and credit standards loosen. The narrative “this time is different” pervades. The language of the period attributes rising prices to fundamental improvements rather than to the self-reinforcing dynamic of credit expansion.

Overtrading occurs as speculation becomes dominant. Assets are bought not for their income streams but for their expected resale prices. Ponzi finance becomes widespread. Leverage reaches levels that can only be sustained by continued price appreciation.

Revulsion is triggered by a specific event, a bank failure, a regulatory announcement, an earnings disappointment, an interest rate rise, that crystallises the realisation that prices have disconnected from fundamentals and that many borrowers are technically insolvent. The trigger is often modest relative to the subsequent collapse: the straw that breaks the camel’s back.

Discredit follows as the rush to exit becomes self-reinforcing. Each actor’s attempt to sell reduces prices, degrading the collateral of others, forcing more selling. Liquidity, which had appeared abundant during the euphoria, evaporates simultaneously for all. The bank run, the margin call, the credit freeze: the mechanisms differ across crises but the dynamic is the same.

15.3 Fisher’s debt deflation spiral

Irving Fisher (1933) identified the self-reinforcing mechanism that converts a financial crisis into a depression. When asset prices fall, agent net worth deteriorates, forcing asset sales to maintain leverage ratios, which drives prices down further, degrading net worth again. Each step feeds the next in a self-sustaining downward spiral.

The mechanism has seven steps: over-indebtedness leads to distress selling; distress selling leads to asset price contraction; asset price contraction leads to a fall in the price level; the price level fall leads to a rise in the real value of debts; the rise in real debt leads to further distress selling; further distress selling leads to further price contraction; and so on until the cycle is broken by external intervention or bankruptcy elimination.

Fisher wrote in 1933 that “the more the debtors pay, the more they owe.” This is the paradox of deleveraging: individually rational attempts to reduce debt are collectively self-defeating because they simultaneously reduce income and asset prices, making the debt burden larger in real terms. The policy implication that Fisher drew, and that Keynes, Minsky, and Bernanke all confirmed from different starting points, is that only an external actor capable of expanding balance sheets (the state, the central bank) can break the spiral. Allowing it to run to exhaustion produces depressions.

Why this framework matters for Morocco now

Morocco’s real estate market exhibited the Minsky five-phase sequence with textbook precision between 2006 and 2014. The displacement: post-2006 Casablanca and Marrakech saw demand surge driven by MRE remittances, demographic growth, and optimistic developer projections. The euphoria: prices doubled in several cities between 2006 and 2013; developers Addoha and Alliances expanded massively on debt, banks loosened mortgage criteria, and “real estate only goes up” became conventional wisdom. The overtrading: developers could no longer service debt from housing sale revenues alone, survival depended on continued price appreciation (textbook Ponzi finance). The revulsion: prices stagnated and declined from 2014; developer balance sheets deteriorated; Addoha and Alliances faced acute financial stress requiring restructuring. Morocco avoided systemic crisis partly through domestic financing structure (limiting international contagion channels) and partly through prudential vigilance. But the sequence was Minsky’s, not an accident.

16 Part VI — Eight Major Crises

16.1 The 1929 crash and the Great Depression: the archetype

The 1929 Wall Street crash and the subsequent Great Depression remain the definitive reference for understanding financial crises and their real consequences. The crash itself, a 89% peak-to-trough decline in the Dow Jones between 1929 and 1932, was dramatic but would not have produced a depression without the amplification mechanisms that converted it into a decade of economic catastrophe.

The crash triggered exactly Fisher’s debt deflation spiral: collapsing asset prices destroyed household and firm net worth, forcing balance sheet contraction, reducing aggregate demand, depressing prices, increasing the real burden of debts, and producing a downward spiral. The Federal Reserve’s contractionary monetary policy, allowing the money supply to fall by a third, amplified the contraction rather than cushioning it. Bank runs eliminated a third of US banks, destroying the payment system and the credit-creation mechanism simultaneously. The Smoot-Hawley tariffs triggered retaliatory protectionism that collapsed world trade.

The Great Depression produced the institutional architecture that governed finance for forty years: deposit insurance (eliminating the incentive for bank runs), the Glass-Steagall separation of commercial and investment banking, the Securities Exchange Commission, and the Bretton Woods monetary framework. The lesson, that financial instability has catastrophic real consequences and that public intervention is indispensable, was the intellectual foundation of the post-war regulatory settlement.

16.2 The Japanese asset bubble of the 1990s: balance sheet deflation

Japan’s experience after its asset bubble collapsed in 1990-1991 introduced a new concept: balance sheet deflation and the zero lower bound. The Bank of Japan had allowed land and stock prices to triple during the late 1980s, fuelled by explosive credit growth. When the bubble burst, Japanese banks were left with massive non-performing loans secured by overvalued real estate collateral. The Bank of Japan cut rates to zero; they stayed there. The government launched a decade of fiscal stimulus programmes totalling over 100 trillion yen.

Yet growth remained anaemic for more than a decade, the “lost decade” that became a lost two decades. Why? Because the private sector was simultaneously deleveraging: firms and households were reducing debt, not increasing spending. Every yen of fiscal stimulus was absorbed by private sector balance sheet repair rather than generating multiplier effects. The Godley sectoral balances analysis is exact here: when the private sector is aggressively saving (reducing debt), only public sector deficits of equivalent magnitude can maintain aggregate demand. Japan ran large deficits but not large enough to fully offset the private deleveraging.

The Japanese experience also illustrated the limits of monetary policy at the zero lower bound, the liquidity trap that IS-LM predicts and that Keynes diagnosed in the 1930s. With nominal rates at zero, conventional monetary policy loses traction. Quantitative easing expanded the monetary base massively; banks held the excess reserves rather than lending. The lesson: monetary policy cannot substitute for fiscal policy when private balance sheets require repair.

16.3 The Asian crisis of 1997: sudden stop and the trilemma

The 1997 East Asian financial crisis demonstrated the sudden stop phenomenon and the impossible trinity in their most destructive form. Thailand, South Korea, Indonesia, and Malaysia had attracted massive foreign capital inflows during the early 1990s, under fixed exchange rate regimes that provided implicit guarantees to foreign investors. The capital flowed into real estate, corporate expansion, and short-term lending in local currencies.

In July 1997, pressure on the Thai baht forced its devaluation. The signal triggered an abrupt reversal of capital flows across the region: investors who had borrowed in dollars to invest in local currencies and assets faced immediate margin calls as exchange rates collapsed. The self-reinforcing dynamic: currency depreciation raised the domestic currency cost of dollar debts, creating further insolvency pressure, driving further selling, driving further depreciation.

The IMF’s initial response, imposing fiscal austerity and high interest rates to defend exchange rates, was later acknowledged to have been counterproductive, deepening recessions precisely when expansionary policy was needed. The crisis demonstrated that under capital mobility, fixed exchange rates are vulnerable to speculative attacks that can be self-fulfilling regardless of economic fundamentals. It provided the most dramatic evidence for Rodrik’s trilemma (developed in Chapter 11): deep capital account integration is fundamentally incompatible with macroeconomic policy autonomy.

16.4 The 1994 Mexican crisis: the first crisis of financial globalisation

The December 1994 Mexican peso crisis introduced the concept of the “tequila effect”, the rapid spread of a currency crisis across countries with no fundamental relationship to Mexico’s specific vulnerabilities. Mexico had attracted massive portfolio inflows during the early 1990s under a fixed exchange rate regime, taking advantage of the North American Free Trade Agreement’s positive sentiment. When political shocks (the Zapatista uprising and the assassination of a presidential candidate) combined with a current account deficit exceeding 7% of GDP and declining foreign reserves, investors exited rapidly. The peso was devalued in December 1994 and immediately fell 50% against the dollar.

The contagion to Argentina, Brazil, and other Latin American markets, the tequila effect, had limited fundamental justification: Argentina’s and Brazil’s fiscal positions were not particularly weak relative to Mexico’s. But investors, facing redemption pressures from their US clients, sold Latin American positions indiscriminately to raise liquidity. The mechanism Minsky identified at the firm level, the sudden stop and the liquidity freeze, operated at the sovereign level: creditworthy countries lost market access because investors could not distinguish them from vulnerable ones quickly enough.

The US Treasury and IMF assembled a 50 billion dollar rescue package within weeks, the largest emergency financial intervention since Bretton Woods. The speed and scale of intervention reflected a lesson from history that had not yet been forgotten: the 1930s’ failure to provide international liquidity destroyed the global financial system. The moral hazard concern, that rescue packages encourage future recklessness, was real but judged secondary to the systemic risk of contagion.

16.5 The 2000-2001 dot-com crash: narrative without fundamentals

The technology bubble of 1997-2000 is the purest available illustration of Shiller’s narrative economics. The displacement was real: the internet genuinely transformed communication, commerce, and information. But the transformation was real; the valuations were not. Companies with no revenues and no plausible path to profitability were valued at billions of dollars because the narrative of “winner-take-all internet dominance” justified it. Price-earnings ratios for technology stocks reached 100, 200, 500, levels impossible to justify by any discounted cash flow calculation but comprehensible as expressions of collective narrative.

When the Nasdaq began falling in March 2000, the mechanism was Minsky’s: the first falls revealed the Ponzi structure of financing (venture-backed companies burning cash at unsustainable rates), which triggered further selling, which revealed more Ponzi structures. The Nasdaq lost 78% from peak to trough between 2000 and 2002. But the dotcom crash was contained relative to a banking crisis because most of the speculative investment was in equity rather than debt, losses fell on shareholders rather than on depositors or counterparties.

16.6 The 2007-2008 subprime crisis: the anatomy of a manufactured catastrophe

The 2007-2008 global financial crisis is the most extensively documented Minsky moment of the modern era, and the most instructive for understanding how financial engineering transforms credit risk into systemic catastrophe.

The originate-to-distribute model replaced the traditional bank lending model in which banks retained loans on their balance sheets. Instead, mortgage originators generated loans and immediately sold them to investment banks, which pooled them into Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS) and sold tranches to institutional investors worldwide. Because originators sold the loans immediately, they had weakened incentives to verify borrower quality. Because investors bought tranches rated AAA by rating agencies, they had limited incentive to independently analyse the underlying loan quality. The rating agencies, paid by the securitisation arrangers, competed for mandates partly by accommodating clients’ requests for higher ratings.

CDOs (Collateralised Debt Obligations) took this one step further: pooling the lower-rated tranches of multiple MBS and re-tranching them to generate new AAA-rated securities from predominantly below-investment-grade components. The mathematical modelling underpinning CDO ratings assumed low correlations between mortgage defaults, correlations that proved catastrophically wrong when housing prices declined nationally rather than locally.

Credit Default Swaps were supposed to manage the residual risk. AIG had written hundreds of billions of dollars of CDS insurance on CDOs without maintaining adequate reserves, relying on mathematical models showing that the insured events were so improbable that reserving was unnecessary. When housing prices fell and defaults accumulated, AIG faced obligations it could not meet, threatening simultaneously every institution that had bought protection from it.

The crisis spread because of interconnection opacity: the OTC derivatives market had accumulated hundreds of trillions of notional exposure through bilateral contracts that no regulator or market participant could map comprehensively. Nobody knew who was exposed to what. When Lehman filed for bankruptcy, the question “who holds Lehman’s counterparty risk?” had no clean answer, which made everyone suspect of everyone, freezing interbank lending globally within days.

16.7 The eurozone crisis 2010-2012: sovereign debt and monetary sovereignty

The eurozone crisis that erupted in Greece in 2010 and spread to Ireland, Portugal, Spain, and Italy illustrated a previously underappreciated mechanism: how monetary union without fiscal union creates sovereign debt crises that no individual country can resolve.

Member states of the eurozone had surrendered monetary sovereignty, the ability to create their own currency, while retaining fiscal sovereignty. This combination produced a situation structurally analogous to municipalities with no access to a state government’s printing press. When Greece’s fiscal position deteriorated, it faced the impossible choice facing any agent whose debt is in a currency it cannot create: cut spending to service debt, or default. The ECB’s initial refusal to backstop sovereign debt, on the grounds that this would create moral hazard, allowed bond market contagion to spread from Greece to other periphery sovereigns.

The eventual resolution, Mario Draghi’s July 2012 declaration that the ECB would do “whatever it takes” to preserve the euro, backed by the Outright Monetary Transactions mechanism, worked precisely because it provided the credible central bank backstop that the eurozone architecture had excluded. Draghi’s three words backed by no actual purchases stopped the sovereign debt crisis more effectively than hundreds of billions in bailout funds.

The eurozone crisis is directly relevant for Morocco’s own monetary architecture choices. A country that maintains monetary sovereignty, that issues its own currency, retains the capacity that eurozone peripheral countries lacked: the ability to have the central bank provide liquidity to the government in extremis. Morocco’s dirham, Bank Al-Maghrib’s operational independence, and the gradual exchange rate liberalisation all preserve this capacity. The Greek experience illustrates its value.

16.8 The four cross-crisis regularities

Across these six crises, four regularities emerge that constitute a diagnostic toolkit for any contemporary financial analyst.

First regularity: magical thinking precedes every crisis. “Housing prices cannot fall.” “East Asia has a different growth model.” “The dollar peg is irreversible.” “Securitisation has dispersed risk.” Every bubble constructs a collective belief that “this time is different”, documented by Reinhart and Rogoff across eight centuries of financial history. The specific content changes; the cognitive structure is identical.

Second regularity: crises propagate through balance sheets and interconnections, not only through fundamentals. A financially sound country can be destroyed by a crisis it did not cause, simply by belonging to the same asset category as fragile countries. Creditworthiness is a collective assessment, and its loss is collective. The contagion from Thailand to Korea in 1997 had little to do with Korean fundamentals; it had everything to do with the “emerging Asia” narrative that linked them in investor portfolios.

Third regularity: policy responses matter as much as the crisis itself. Procyclical austerity during recession amplifies the downswing. Liquidity injection into fundamentally insolvent institutions delays and amplifies eventual collapse. The quality of diagnosis, liquidity crisis versus solvency crisis, demand shock versus supply shock, determines response effectiveness. The IMF’s Asian crisis response and the ECB’s early eurozone response both illustrate how incorrect diagnosis produces harmful intervention.

Fourth regularity: crises reveal institutional fragilities accumulated during prosperity. Insufficient supervision, unresolved conflicts of interest, implicit public guarantees not offset by prudential constraints, governance deficits. Calm periods are the time to build the institutions that will resist the next crisis, not to relax the vigilance that the previous crisis made seem unnecessary.

17 Part VII — Morocco’s Financial System

17.1 Three forms of Moroccan capitalism

Understanding Morocco’s financial system requires first understanding the structure of Moroccan capitalism itself, which combines three distinct forms with different governance logics and different relationships to financial markets.

Patrimonial family capitalism is the most widespread form beyond large listed firms. Business families control diversified conglomerates through pyramidal structures: a family holding controls an intermediate holding that controls subsidiaries in real estate, agri-food, distribution, and light industry. Effective control is exercised with minority participation through leverage effects of nested structures. The Sefrioui group (Douja Prom), the Chaâbi group (Banque Populaire, construction), and the Benjelloun group (BMCE Bank of Africa) illustrate this configuration. This capitalism values discretion, interpersonal loyalty, and long-term trust networks. Growth is financed through retained earnings and bank debt from allied family banks rather than through the stock market, which requires transparency, disclosure obligations, and possible challenges to control that families structurally avoid.

Managerialist capitalism of public enterprises is incarnated by OCP, ONEE, ONCF, ADM, and financial institutions where the state holds indirect stakes via Al Mada and CDG. These enterprises operate through professionalised managerial logic, technocratic executives, formalised governance processes, published performance indicators, sometimes subject to international bond market discipline. OCP’s transformation from raw phosphate exporter to integrated fertiliser producer to African agri-nutrition actor illustrates the developmental potential of this model. Its risk: public enterprises may pursue political objectives at the expense of economic efficiency, and their executives respond to political principals with shorter horizons than industrial investment requires.

The CDG’s institutional financial capitalism is the least visible but perhaps the most structurally significant. The Caisse de Dépôt et de Gestion manages public social fund reserves and invests across all asset classes, listed equities, bonds, real estate, infrastructure, private equity, with a long horizon and a risk-adjusted return logic. The CDG plays a structural stabilisation role: as a long-term shareholder in listed companies, it provides continuous demand that limits market volatility; as a structural buyer of Treasury bonds, it contributes to bond market liquidity; through its subsidiaries (CIH Bank, infrastructure funds), it finances projects that neither private markets nor the public budget would finance alone.

17.2 Panorama of Morocco’s financial system

The banking sector is the core. Fourteen licensed banks, dominated by three groups: Attijariwafa Bank (the leading French-speaking African bank), Banque Centrale Populaire (a mutualist network with strong SME penetration), and Bank of Africa, BMCE (strong sub-Saharan presence). These three groups concentrate more than 65% of total banking assets. Bank credit to the private sector represents approximately 70-75% of GDP, above the sub-Saharan African average but below levels in Turkey (~100%) or Malaysia (~130%).

The Casablanca Stock Exchange (BVC) groups approximately 76 listed companies. Its total capitalisation represents approximately 40-45% of GDP, a decent level by regional comparison but structurally concentrated. The top ten MASI companies represent over 70% of total capitalisation. Below these blue chips lies a thin mid-cap market with insufficient liquidity to attract institutional investors, and a minimal small-cap segment.

The bond market is more active than the equity market. Treasury bonds, auctioned weekly through Primary Dealers (Intermédiaires en Valeurs du Trésor), define the risk-free yield curve, with an outstanding amount exceeding 550 billion MAD in 2023. Corporate bonds exist but are concentrated among large issuers: OCP, ONCF, CDG, and banks. Sukuk (Islamic bonds) have been developing since 2018, providing an alternative financing instrument aligned with Islamic finance principles.

17.3 Islamic finance: an alternative architecture

Islamic finance is not simply a prohibition on interest. It is a financing system built on alternative principles: risk-sharing between financier and financed, anchoring in real assets, prohibition of pure speculation (gharar), and alignment of financial returns with real economic activity.

The five principal instruments operationalise these principles. Mourabaha (cost-plus sale) is the most widespread: the bank buys an asset at the client’s request and immediately resells it at a higher price payable in instalments, generating a return from trade rather than lending. Ijara (leasing) allows the use of an asset against regular payments, with ownership remaining with the bank. Mousharaka (partnership) is a joint venture where the bank contributes capital and shares profits and losses proportionally. Moudaraba (silent partnership) separates capital provision (bank) from management expertise (entrepreneur), with profit-sharing and loss borne by capital unless negligence is proven. Sukuk are certificates structured as shared ownership of tangible assets generating returns, rather than interest-bearing bonds.

Morocco authorised participatory banks (the Moroccan term for Islamic banks) in 2017. By 2023, five participatory banks were operating (CIH Bank Participatif, Umnia Bank, Al Akhdar Bank, BTI Bank, Bank Al-Yousr), with cumulative financing above 30 billion MAD. Their growth responds to genuine demand from segments of the population that declined conventional banking on religious grounds, representing a real financial inclusion gain, not merely a product differentiation.

17.4 Structural weaknesses and the development financing gap

Four structural weaknesses limit the financial system’s contribution to Morocco’s development.

Incomplete financial inclusion: despite progress (approximately 65% banking rate), significant disparities persist between urban and rural areas, between men and women, and between formal and informal employment. Al Barid Bank’s postal network and participatory banks partially address these gaps, but approximately 35% of the adult population remains unbanked.

Insufficient credit access for SMEs: these enterprises represent 95% of Morocco’s productive fabric but capture less than 25% of business bank credit. TAMWILCOM (formerly CCG, Caisse Centrale de Garantie) provides public guarantees to reduce banks’ perceived risk, a documented mechanism for correcting the information asymmetry and collateral gap that prevents SME financing. But the guarantee instruments are underutilised relative to need, and the administrative requirements for accessing them remain burdensome for small enterprises.

Weak stock market culture: Moroccan household savings are massively oriented toward real estate and bank deposits. The perception of the stock exchange as opaque and reserved for insiders discourages the vast majority of savers. The absence of simple, low-cost investment products, accessible index funds, popular equity savings plans, perpetuates this compartmentalisation and limits the BVC’s role as a savings and allocation mechanism.

Pyramidal structures and governance: family groups controlling conglomerates through multi-level structures limit transparency and protect strategic decisions from external challenge. Minority shareholders, particularly foreign institutional investors, have limited recourse against management decisions favouring controlling shareholders. This reality deters foreign investors and limits the depth and liquidity of the stock market, which is ultimately why Morocco’s ECI (Economic Complexity Index) scores are lower than its income level would predict.

17.5 The financialisation of Moroccan capitalism and its limits

Al Mada’s trajectory illustrates the Moroccan face of financialisation. Born in industry and commerce as the ONA conglomerate, it has progressively pivoted toward financial participation management. Its value resides primarily in its stakes in Attijariwafa Bank, Cosumar, Maroc Telecom, and other companies rather than in proprietary productive activity. Al Mada optimises dividend flows between subsidiaries and deploys capital in African participations, a logic more financial than productive.

This trajectory is not unique to Morocco: it reflects the global shift, documented by Aglietta and Boyer’s Regulation School, from “Fordist” accumulation regimes based on productive investment and wage growth to “financialised” accumulation regimes based on asset appreciation and financial portfolio management. For Morocco’s development objectives, this creates a specific tension: the country needs capital deployed in productive industrial investment and capability-building, but the dominant logic of its largest private capital holder is portfolio optimisation rather than productive transformation. This tension is not easily resolved through market mechanisms, it requires the institutional interventions (conditional reciprocity, performance-linked public support) that industrial policy seeks to provide.

18 Conclusion: Finance in the Service of Development, or Development in the Service of Finance?

This chapter has built the full architecture of financial markets and fragility, from the foundational nature of financial assets as promises to the regularities of eight major crises.

Three conclusions structure everything that follows.

Finance is indispensable and structurally unstable, both, simultaneously and irreducibly. The same mechanisms that allow societies to finance productive investment beyond their current means also generate the periodic collapses that destroy that investment. There is no design of the financial system that eliminates this tension; there is only better or worse management of it, through institutions that create buffers, limit leverage, enforce transparency, and provide the lender of last resort function that prevents individual failures from cascading into systemic collapse.

Understanding financial markets requires simultaneously understanding their efficiency and their irrationality. Fama’s efficient market hypothesis is a useful benchmark against which to measure actual market behaviour, not a description of actual market behaviour. Behavioural finance, narrative economics, and Minsky’s endogenous instability all document systematic, predictable departures from efficiency that any serious analyst must account for.

Morocco’s financial development is both an achievement and a constraint. The banking system is well-capitalised and relatively stable by regional standards. The BVC provides an equity market with genuine price discovery. Bank Al-Maghrib’s operational independence and prudential supervision have prevented the systemic crises that comparable economies have experienced. But the financial system’s contribution to Morocco’s productive transformation, financing SMEs, deepening capital markets, enabling long-horizon industrial investment, remains far below its potential. The binding constraint is not the quantity of financial resources but their allocation: the institutional design of the system that directs capital toward real estate rather than manufacturing, toward large connected firms rather than innovative SMEs, toward dividend extraction rather than productive reinvestment. These allocation failures are not market imperfections to be corrected at the margin; they are structural features of the three forms of Moroccan capitalism that require the industrial policy interventions analysed in Chapters 10 and 12.

Three specific reforms would most directly address Morocco’s financial development gaps. First, creating a growth market segment on the BVC with simplified listing requirements, reduced disclosure costs, and dedicated market-making arrangements for mid-cap firms would break the circular logic that keeps the market illiquid: companies do not list because the market is illiquid; the market is illiquid because few diverse issuers list. Second, restructuring TAMWILCOM’s guarantee instruments to be automatically available against standardised SME risk profiles rather than requiring bespoke administrative evaluation for each guarantee would dramatically expand credit access for small productive firms. Third, developing accessible retail investment products, low-cost OPCVM equity index funds tracking the MASI, accessible through Al Barid Bank’s postal network, would allow ordinary Moroccan savers to participate in the productive economy’s returns rather than concentrating exclusively in real estate and term deposits. None of these reforms requires dismantling the structures of Moroccan capitalism; they require using public institutional capacity to correct the specific allocation failures that those structures generate.

What this chapter should leave with the reader

A financial asset is a promise on uncertain future flows, not a present good. Markets organise the circulation of these promises. This makes financial prices sensitive to collective narratives and structurally prone to bubbles.

The four functions of finance, savings channelling, risk allocation, asset pricing, maturity transformation, are real but regularly perverted. The central question is not “is finance useful?” but “under what institutional conditions does it serve the real economy?”

Market efficiency is a hypothesis in three forms (weak, semi-strong, strong), not a description of actual markets. Behavioural finance documents systematic deviations; Shiller’s narrative economics explains them through viral story propagation.

Minsky’s cycle is the anatomy of every financial crisis: displacement → euphoria → overtrading → revulsion → discredit. The key insight: stability engenders instability. This cycle is not accidental, it is the endogenous product of competitive financial dynamics.

Fisher’s debt deflation spiral shows how individually rational deleveraging is collectively catastrophic: the more debtors pay, the more they owe. Only an external actor expanding its balance sheet can break the spiral.

The four cross-crisis regularities: magical thinking precedes every crisis; contagion propagates through balance sheets; policy responses matter as much as the initial shock; crises reveal institutional fragilities accumulated during prosperity.

Morocco’s three capitalisms, patrimonial family, managerialist public, CDG institutional, each have distinctive relationships to financial markets. The binding constraint on Morocco’s financial development is not the quantity of capital but its allocation away from productive transformation.
