18  Chapter 16 — International Political Economy

Markets, states, and power relations

“International politics is, like all politics, a struggle for power.” — Hans J. Morgenthau, Politics Among Nations (1948)

“Structural power is the power to shape the frameworks within which others must operate.” — Susan Strange, States and Markets (1988)

The guiding thread of this chapter
  1. The world economy is a political order. Markets presuppose secure trade routes, accepted currencies, enforceable contracts, recognised standards, and mechanisms of coercion. This order is not natural; it is produced and defended by powers.
  2. Structural power exceeds relational power. The capacity to shape the frameworks within which all actors must operate, currencies, standards, infrastructure, technology, narratives, is more decisive than the capacity to directly compel a specific actor.
  3. Interdependence can be weaponised. Asymmetric dependence allows whoever controls a network’s central nodes, the dollar, SWIFT, platforms, semiconductors, rating agencies, maritime freight, to use that position as a lever of economic coercion.
  4. Standards are power. Whoever sets the standards sets the conditions of market access. Cognitive dependence, adopting others’ categories and evaluation criteria, is a form of dependence in its own right.

The Map

Where we stand. The course has moved through the mechanisms of the economy from the standpoint of agents, firms, households, banks, states, and their interactions at different scales. The chapters on international trade and the balance of payments analysed flows, comparative advantage, exchange-rate regimes, and external constraints. This chapter shifts the angle of vision. It no longer asks only how exchange happens, but within what political order it operates. International markets do not exist in a vacuum. They exist inside an order made of rules, institutions, infrastructure, currencies, secured routes, and power relations.

This chapter introduces the major theories that allow us to think of the world economy as a political space. Its ambition is not to teach international relations as a discipline. It is to give economists the missing concepts needed to read a trade decision, a sanction, an investment agreement, an environmental standard, or a port project without naivety.

The chapter follows a cumulative logic. It begins by establishing that the world economy is a structured political order, not a neutral market. It examines the two great analytical traditions, realism and liberal institutionalism. It introduces the concepts of hegemony and structural power. It analyses how interdependence is converted into an economic weapon, and the instruments of contemporary economic warfare, sanctions, technology controls, infrastructure cartels. It closes with constructivist approaches and a method for strategic reading.

Morocco is the standing test case: every abstract concept must be usable to read a concrete decision involving Morocco, a free-trade agreement, a foreign investment, a European standard, an IMF loan, or an African infrastructure project.

The World Economy as a Political Order

The foundational thesis: markets presuppose politics

Begin with a question that international economics textbooks rarely ask: why does international trade work at all? Not in the technical sense of comparative advantage, but in the practical sense: how does a container of automotive components leave Shanghai, cross the Strait of Malacca, pass through the Suez Canal, arrive at Tanger Med, and reach a factory in Kénitra, without anyone being robbed along the way, without contracts being violated, without currencies collapsing?

The answer is that an order exists. That order is made of maritime routes secured by naval forces, trade rules negotiated under the WTO, a payment system anchored on the dollar and the SWIFT network, port infrastructure financed by states or sovereign funds, intellectual property rights defended by treaties and courts, technical standards set by standardisation bodies, and maritime insurance conventions codified since the eighteenth century.

None of these elements is spontaneous. All are the product of political decisions, agreements among states, stabilised power relations, technological choices, historical inheritances. The real world economy is therefore not the flat, homogeneous market of the textbooks. It is a hierarchical space, unequally accessible, crossed by power asymmetries and structural dependencies.

Susan Strange coined the most useful formulation: there is a fundamental difference between relational power, the capacity to force a specific actor to do something it would not otherwise do, and structural power, the capacity to shape the frameworks within which all actors operate. To control rules, currencies, technologies, infrastructure, and standards is to exercise structural power: even without direct coercion, others must conform to the frameworks one has built.

The Tangier container: a chain of invisible dependencies

An auto-parts supplier in Tangier ships components to a Renault plant in France. This routine transaction mobilises an invisible political infrastructure: a bill of lading standardised under international conventions, marine insurance priced in pounds sterling at Lloyd’s of London, a euro payment clearing through the Belgium-based SWIFT network, a shipping lane secured by the American navy in the Mediterranean, unloading at Barcelona under EU port standards, customs clearance under the 2000 Morocco-EU free-trade agreement, and Renault’s own technical specifications that the Moroccan supplier must meet. Remove any one of these elements and the transaction no longer happens. The political order is not a superstructure added onto the economy, it is the economy’s precondition.

Three analytical levels of this order should be distinguished. The first is the level of transactions: firms, banks, consumers, investors. The second is the level of rules: trade agreements, standards, financial institutions, investment regimes, intellectual property law. The third is the level of power: dominant states, military alliances, control of critical infrastructure, sanctioning capacity, technological mastery, monetary hegemony. These three levels are interlocked, transactions unfold within the framework of rules, and rules form within the context of power.

Realism: Power, Security, and Interstate Rivalry

International anarchy and its economic consequences

Realism starts from a sober observation about the structure of the international system: no world government exists to protect states from one another. Unlike inside a state, where coercive institutions, courts, police, armies, can enforce rules, the international system is anarchic, not chaotic, but lacking central authority. In this setting, each state must ultimately rely on itself for security.

Hans Morgenthau, the father of classical realism, draws a direct conclusion: international politics is a struggle for power. Kenneth Waltz refines this intuition in neorealism: it is the structure of the international system (bipolar, unipolar, multipolar) that constrains behaviour, regardless of actors’ intentions. John Mearsheimer pushes the logic further in offensive realism: great powers do not merely seek survival, they seek to maximise their relative share of world power.

Why realism matters to economists

From a realist perspective, certain economic exchanges are not treated as ordinary commercial transactions, they become national security questions: advanced semiconductor production, weapons manufacturing, energy supply, port control, data access, cloud computing. When the United States restricts NVIDIA chip exports to China, when the EU scrutinises foreign acquisitions of port operators, when Morocco reserves certain infrastructure for domestic ownership, realist economics is at work. The question is not “is this efficient?” but “is this safe?” The two questions can have different answers.

The practical lesson of realism for economists is threefold. Relative gains matter as much as absolute gains: an agreement economically beneficial to both parties can be rejected if one side judges that it strengthens its rival more. Certain sectors are strategic and cannot be treated as ordinary commodities. Dependence is a risk: every relationship of interdependence also creates a potential vulnerability that an adversary can exploit.

🇲🇦 Phosphate as a geopolitical lever

OCP holds three-quarters of the world’s phosphate reserves, the irreplaceable input of agricultural fertilisation. This near-monopoly position is a geopolitical asset of exceptional power: without phosphate, no fertiliser; without fertiliser, no intensive agriculture capable of feeding ten billion people. Morocco grasps this realist dimension intuitively, hence the classification of phosphate as a “national strategic resource” exempted from ordinary trade rules.

But the power of phosphate is only real if OCP climbs the value chain, from raw rock to phosphoric acid, from basic fertilisers to complex formulations, from selling products to providing agronomic advice. A monopoly on raw rock is less powerful than a monopoly on plant-nutrition solutions. The realist view holds that power is worth what you institutionally make of it.

Liberal Institutionalism: Interdependence, Cooperation, and Rules

Cooperation is possible, and it lasts

Liberal institutionalism acknowledges international anarchy but disputes that it inevitably leads to rivalry. States have absolute interests in cooperating, and these interests can be stabilised by institutions that reduce uncertainty, supply information, and lower transaction costs.

Robert Keohane shows in After Hegemony (1984) that international cooperation can persist even after the decline of the hegemonic power that originally structured it, provided that solid institutions have been built. International regimes function as collective memory: they recall past commitments, make violations reputationally costly, and facilitate repeated coordination.

Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye introduce complex interdependence: international relations no longer run only through military channels. They also run through multinational corporations, financial flows, migration, international organisations, culture, media, and NGOs. This complexity makes states more vulnerable, but it also reduces incentives for war: a country that trades massively with another has an interest in keeping the peace with it.

The WTO as an international regime: strengths and limits

The WTO is an international regime in Krasner’s sense: a set of principles (free trade, non-discrimination), norms (most-favoured-nation treatment, national treatment), rules (bound tariffs, anti-dumping procedures), and procedures (the dispute settlement mechanism). This regime has facilitated a considerable expansion of world trade since 1995. But it reflects the power relations at the moment of its creation. Intellectual property rules (TRIPS) were negotiated to the advantage of patent-holding countries. Agricultural rules let rich countries maintain subsidies that poor countries cannot afford. For Morocco, the WTO is simultaneously a protection, the rules limit partners’ arbitrariness, and a constraint, certain industrial or food policies are difficult to sustain within this framework.

A crucial point: international institutions are not neutral. They reflect the power relations of the moment of their creation. The IMF, the World Bank, and the GATT were designed in the post-war period under American leadership, reflecting the interests and preferences of the United States of that era. For developing countries, this duality is constantly experienced: the IMF can help a country in a liquidity crisis, and it can also impose conditionalities that serve creditors’ interests more than the interests of the country being helped.

Multilateralism has been in a deep crisis since the 2010s. The WTO’s Appellate Body has been paralysed since 2019. Climate negotiations move slowly. IMF reform to give more weight to emerging countries advances at the pace allowed by dominant shareholders. This paralysis does not mean the end of institutions, they continue to function on hundreds of technical files. It means that the great ambitions of liberal multilateralism collide with the return of great-power rivalry.

Hegemony, Stability, and Structural Power

Hegemonic stability theory

Charles Kindleberger, analysing the Great Depression, formulates a central thesis: the stability of the world economy requires a dominant power both capable of and willing to supply certain international public goods, lender-of-last-resort liquidity, an open import market in times of crisis, stable exchange rates, macroeconomic coordination. In the absence of such a stabiliser, the international economic system tends toward beggar-thy-neighbour behaviour and instability.

Robert Gilpin deepens this idea: the post-war liberal economic order was made possible by American hegemony. The United States had the capacity (the world’s largest economy, the dollar as reserve currency, a navy projecting power along trade routes) and the will (an interest in stabilising Western capitalism against the Soviet threat) to supply these public goods. But hegemony also confers considerable advantages on the dominant power: the dollar’s exorbitant privilege (borrowing cheaply in one’s own currency), institutional influence, the capacity to impose financial sanctions, preferential access to information.

Susan Strange’s four structures of power

Susan Strange proposes a remarkable analytical grid. She distinguishes four primary structures whose control confers power over all actors.

The security structure: who can guarantee or threaten others’ security?

The production structure: who controls what is produced, where, how, and on what terms? Mastery of value chains, technologies, industrial standards, and intellectual property confers power over the entire world economy.

The finance structure: who can create and allocate credit? The power of finance, banks, markets, reserve currencies, payment systems, shapes the conditions under which all other actors operate.

The knowledge structure: who controls access to communications, information, technology, and ideas? This structure becomes central in the digital economy and technological rivalry.

For a country like Morocco, this means that dependencies are not only commercial (importing oil and wheat), but also financial (refinancing in foreign currency), technological (operating systems, cloud, semiconductors), and normative (European standards that Moroccan exporters must meet).

Weaponised Interdependence: Sanctions, Extraterritoriality, and Economic Warfare

When interdependence becomes a weapon

Interdependence is often presented as a force for peace. That thesis is correct, but incomplete. Interdependence also creates asymmetric vulnerabilities that dominant actors can convert into levers of coercion.

Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman conceptualised this phenomenon as weaponised interdependence. Global economic networks have central nodes, infrastructure, platforms, currencies, markets, technologies, standards, through which most flows pass. Whoever controls these nodes can exercise two kinds of power: a panopticon effect (observing all flows that pass through) and a chokepoint effect (blocking access for non-compliant actors).

Dollar centrality in international payments gives the United States both effects simultaneously. The SWIFT network, headquartered in Belgium but operating under effective American influence over dollar clearing, allows Washington to exclude targeted banks and countries from the global payment system. The 2022 sanctions excluding major Russian banks from SWIFT, following the invasion of Ukraine, demonstrated the reach of this instrument: a coordinated decision by a handful of governments cut off a G20 economy’s banking sector from most of the world’s payment infrastructure within days.

Supply chains constitute a second weaponisable infrastructure. Whoever controls a critical input, a component, a technology, a raw material, with few substitutes can leverage that control as a geopolitical instrument. American restrictions on advanced semiconductor and lithography equipment exports to China since 2022 illustrate this logic precisely: by controlling ASML’s EUV lithography machines and the design software needed to produce advanced chips, the United States and its allies can constrain China’s technological trajectory without firing a shot.

The contemporary instruments of economic warfare

Beyond sanctions and export controls, contemporary economic warfare deploys a widening toolkit. Industrial subsidies as competitive weapons: the American CHIPS Act (2022, $52 billion) and the European Chips Act (2023, €43 billion) use public money to reshape where critical industries locate, converting fiscal capacity into geopolitical positioning. Investment screening: most major economies now screen foreign investment in critical sectors, ports, telecommunications, semiconductors, energy, treating capital flows as security questions rather than purely commercial ones. Standard-setting as a chokepoint: control over technical standards, 5G protocols, AI governance frameworks, payment messaging standards, determines which firms and countries can participate in future markets.

For middle powers like Morocco, the practical lesson is that integration into global supply chains is never purely commercial. The choice of telecommunications vendor, cloud provider, or payment infrastructure carries strategic implications that a purely cost-based procurement logic will miss.

The Brussels effect: regulation as structural power

Anu Bradford identifies a distinctive mechanism through which the European Union exercises structural power without military or even primarily financial means: the Brussels effect. Because the EU is a market too large for most global firms to ignore, and because compliance costs are often lower when applied uniformly across all markets than maintained separately for the EU alone, firms frequently adopt EU standards globally rather than maintaining EU-specific production lines. The General Data Protection Regulation became a de facto global privacy standard not because the EU coerced anyone, but because compliance with the strictest standard was commercially simpler than fragmentation.

🇲🇦 The Brussels effect and Moroccan exporters

For Morocco, a massive exporter to the EU, this effect is concrete and constant. Moroccan tomatoes exported to Europe must meet European phytosanitary standards. Cars assembled in Kénitra for the European market must meet European emissions standards. The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), now being phased in, will tax imports according to their carbon footprint, forcing accelerated decarbonisation of Morocco’s exporting industry regardless of any domestic political decision.

This normative dimension reveals a permanent tension for developing countries. International standards can be instruments of protection, learning, and exclusion simultaneously. The strategic question is not whether to reject standards, it is how to participate actively in their design, negotiate adaptation timelines, and use compliance effort as an engine of productive upgrading.

Norms, Ideas, and Legitimacy: What Realism Does Not Always See

The power of ideas and norms

Constructivism, associated with Alexander Wendt, argues that international structures are not purely material, they are also made of shared ideas, identities, and norms. “Anarchy is what states make of it,” in Wendt’s phrase: the same structural condition (no world government) can produce rivalry or cooperation depending on the intersubjective understandings that states build over time.

John Ruggie introduces the concept of embedded liberalism: the post-war international economic order combined trade openness with extensive domestic social protections, a compromise between liberal internationalism and the political need to shield societies from market volatility. This embedding has eroded since the 1980s as financial liberalisation outpaced the construction of compensating domestic safety nets, a tension visible today in debates over how far Morocco should open in any prospective Deep and Comprehensive Free Trade Agreement with the EU.

Robert Cox distinguishes between problem-solving theory, which works within existing power structures to improve their functioning, and critical theory, which questions the structures themselves and asks whose interests they serve. This distinction matters for reading international economic institutions: are the WTO’s rules a neutral framework to be optimised within, or a structure whose origins and beneficiaries must themselves be interrogated?

For developing countries, legitimacy is not a luxury, it is a political asset. An international order perceived as fair generates more durable compliance than one imposed by sheer power. This is why intermediate countries like Morocco have a structural interest in strengthening multilateral norms and rule-based frameworks, even when they cannot single-handedly determine those rules’ content.

Method: Reading an International Economic Decision

Five questions for moving from description to strategy

Faced with any phenomenon of international economics, a trade agreement, a sanction, a foreign investment, a technical standard, an IMF loan, a port project, an energy agreement, a technology restriction, the tools presented in this chapter allow five questions that transform a descriptive reading into a strategic one.

First question: what is the apparent economic interest? This is the surface reading. Who exports what, at what price, with what value added? Who borrows what, at what rate, for what use? This reading is necessary but insufficient.

Second question: what underlying power relationship is at work? Who initiated this agreement? Who held the greater bargaining power? What conditionalities are attached? Which sectors are excluded? Who defines the dispute-resolution rules?

Third question: what infrastructure is controlled? Who controls the port, the cable, the platform, the currency, the standard, the payment system, the cloud, or the technology at the centre of the transaction? Control of infrastructure creates a durable advantage that individual transactions do not reveal.

Fourth question: what dependency is created or reduced? Every economic relationship creates interdependence. Is it symmetric or asymmetric? Dependence in a critical sector (energy, data, food, payments) is riskier than dependence in a substitutable sector.

Fifth question: what room for manoeuvre remains? Having identified the interests, the power relations, the infrastructure, and the dependencies, what autonomous decision-making capacity does the country concerned retain? Can it renegotiate terms, diversify partners, develop a domestic alternative?

Reading the Morocco-China partnership with this grid

China has become a major trading partner for Morocco, the second-largest source of imports, growing infrastructure investment, and rising interest in phosphates. Apply the five questions.

Apparent interest: Morocco imports competitively priced manufactured goods and attracts infrastructure investment; China gains access to Moroccan phosphates and an African hub. Power relationship: China negotiates from a position of strength with investment capacity Morocco cannot replicate. Infrastructure controlled: if Chinese firms operate ports or industrial zones, this creates a durable structural presence. Dependencies created: dependence on Chinese imports in certain sectors (textiles, electronics) that can weaken domestic industry. Room for manoeuvre: Morocco can leverage its geographic position, European relationships, and stability to diversify partners and negotiate counterparts in technology, training, and local content.

Conclusion: Toward a Strategic Reading of the World Economy

The four great traditions presented in this chapter, realism, liberal institutionalism, hegemonic stability theory, and constructivism, do not exclude one another. They illuminate different aspects of the same reality.

Realism is right to remind us that states seek security in a world without a guarantor. Some sectors will always remain strategic. Industrial policy, supplier diversification, and mastery of critical infrastructure are not archaisms, they are necessities.

Liberal institutionalism is right to show that cooperation is possible and durable. International regimes reduce uncertainty, facilitate exchange, and create mutual gains.

Strange’s theory of structural power is right to point to the world network’s nodes, currency, technology, finance, standards, infrastructure, rating agencies, audit oligopolies, as the decisive sites of real power, invisible to an analysis confined to transactions alone.

Constructivism is right to remind us that norms, identities, and representations also shape behaviour. Legitimacy is not a luxury, it is a political asset.

The contemporary international economic order is in accelerated transition. Geoeconomic fragmentation, the tendency of great powers to reorganise their supply chains along political lines, was already reshaping the geography of investment and world trade by 2025. For Morocco, this fragmentation is an opportunity as much as a risk. The European tendency to diversify sourcing away from Asia creates demand for proximate suppliers, Morocco can position itself as a nearshore industrial hub for Europe in automotive, aerospace, renewable energy, and tomorrow batteries. But this window will not last indefinitely: other countries (Tunisia, Egypt, Turkey, Eastern Europe) are positioning for the same niches. The speed of upgrading and the quality of institutions will be the determining factors.

For Morocco, as for any intermediate country, the conclusion is practical. The world economy is not a space to join naively. It is an order to be analysed, negotiated, and used strategically. Chapter 17 descends into the materiality of this order, ports, routes, resources, cables, and value chains. Chapter 18 proposes a doctrine of insertion for intermediate countries.

What this chapter should leave with the reader

The world economy is a political order. Markets presuppose secure routes, accepted currencies, enforceable contracts, recognised standards, and mechanisms of coercion. This order is not natural, it is produced and defended by powers.

Relational power and structural power. Real power is structural, it operates through currencies, standards, infrastructure, rating agencies, and technological standards, not only through direct coercion.

Interdependence can be weaponised. Asymmetric dependence allows whoever controls the central nodes, the dollar, SWIFT, platforms, semiconductors, to use that position as a coercive lever. Infrastructure oligopolies (the Big Four, rating agencies, stock indices) exercise diffuse structural power without being state actors.

Contemporary economic warfare is systemic. The CHIPS Act, export controls, technological standards, carbon border mechanisms: great powers use their markets, subsidies, and standards as instruments of industrial and geopolitical policy. Intermediate countries must read these instruments to anticipate their effects.

Standards are power. The Brussels effect shows that whoever sets the standards sets the conditions of market access. Cognitive dependence is a form of dependence in its own right.

Five questions for any international decision. Apparent interest, underlying power relationship, infrastructure controlled, dependency created or reduced, remaining room for manoeuvre.

Exercises

Comprehension questions

1. Distinguish Susan Strange’s relational power and structural power. Illustrate each concept with an example drawn from contemporary international economics.

2. Why does realism predict that certain economic sectors will be treated as “strategic” even when free trade would theoretically be more efficient? Give three current sectoral examples.

3. Explain Farrell and Newman’s concept of weaponised interdependence. How does it differ from Keohane and Nye’s classical interdependence? Illustrate with the SWIFT sanctions against Russia.

4. What is the Brussels effect? How does it illustrate structural power without direct coercion? How is the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism a new-generation example of it?

5. How do rating agencies (Moody’s, S&P, Fitch) and stock indices (MSCI) exercise structural power over developing economies? How does this illustrate the notion of “private structural power”?

Case study — Morocco and ALECA

The European Union has proposed negotiations with Morocco for a Deep and Comprehensive Free Trade Agreement (ALECA) that would extend the 2000 agreement to agriculture, services, and investment rules.

a) Apply the five questions of strategic reading to this agreement. Who initiated it? Which sectors are included and excluded? What normative infrastructure is at stake? What dependencies are created or reduced?

b) How does Ruggie’s concept of embedded liberalism apply here? What social or industrial protection mechanisms should Morocco negotiate to “embed” trade opening?

c) Drawing on Strange, in which structure of power does this agreement primarily fit, security, production, finance, or knowledge?

Reflection question — Morocco’s critical dependencies

Build a dependency matrix for Morocco by identifying five current critical dependencies (energy, food, technology, finance, data, or others). For each: the nature of the dependency, its degree of criticality, the main partner, available alternatives, and a feasible vulnerability-reduction strategy. Include in your analysis at least one dependency in Strange’s “knowledge” structure (standards, benchmarks, rankings, consulting).
